# Scenario development

Scenario development is one of the main concepts and most widely used methods in foresight. Both public and private sector organisations have implemented scenarios for a wide array of functions to support better longer-term strategic anticipation.

## How it works

- Scenario planning offers a structured approach that describes a plausible set of future conditions that are different to the present.
- Scenarios are always developed as a set, together mapping out a broad outcome space that allows different futures to be contrasted with one another and with the present, helping to break away from a deterministic view of the future. This allows organisations to consider alternative outcomes representing novel perspectives and contexts, as well as discontinuities, that may be difficult to grasp in the present.
- It is a process that involves both analysis and storytelling to craft credible and engaging images of the future that provoke decision-makers to think differently.
- Scenarios enable this by always adopting an 'outside-in' approach, focusing on potential changes in an organisation's external environment, including outcomes 'beyond the numbers' that might otherwise be overlooked. This, in turn, influences the organisation's strategic environment and consequently strategic decisions.
- In this way, scenarios provide a common outset and a basis for a well-informed discussion of future possibilities and challenges, enabling organisations to widen their strategic perspective and approach the future with curiosity, readiness and vision.
- There are different scenario development methods, two of which are described below. Both these approaches can vary in their level of detail and the resources required, depending on how extensively the process is scoped.

## 2 x 2 scenarios

- This approach is often used to build scenarios with a 'decision focus' and strategic relevance. It starts from identifying critical uncertainties in relation to the future in focus and then leverages these uncertainties to craft alternative futures.
- It is arguably the most extensive approach to scenario building and generally implies a step-by-step process that takes you through research, driver mapping and uncertainties assessment over a series of participatory workshops.
- To start building the actual scenarios, there is initially a need to identify critical uncertainties (see Uncertainties Assessment) that are combined as two axes of uncertainty in a 2 x 2 matrix. You should decide on an axis combination that will create the most interesting or valuable scenario matrix.
- As a rule, the two axes must be independent of each other, so that they don't collapse.
- Four 'scenario-spaces' emerge from combining the two axes of uncertainty, and each 'scenario-space' is then elaborated into complete narratives that consider relevant drivers and highlight key characteristics to make them relevant for decision-making.
- You should make sure events and characteristics in the scenarios are plausible and logically consistent. Also make sure that the four scenarios are structurally or qualitatively different.

## Driver-based scenarios

- Rather than organising scenarios around critical uncertainties and a 2 x 2 matrix, the driver-based approach anchors each scenario in a different core driver.
- You select a core driver, or a small cluster of drivers, to serve as a scenario's anchor, the thing that dominates and gives the world its character, and then build outward, working through how everything else behaves in the scenario given that driver's prominence. Which drivers can carry a world is a judgement made as part of the process, taking into account what is relevant to the project at hand.
- Contrast between the scenarios therefore comes from which driver dominates. This makes the set considerably more flexible, as driver-based scenarios are each constructed on their own terms and are not structurally locked to one another in a matrix.
- There are a few reasons to pursue this alternative approach. Most simply, it can move directly from driver mapping to scenario development, without requiring an uncertainties assessment first. It also avoids a common weakness of the 2 x 2, which sometimes invites 'lazy' axis polarity definition, crossing axes that are unidirectional, such as fast/slow or high/low polarities, leaving you with four relatively uninteresting and 'flat' scenarios, with little room for world-building or for properly exploring the focal issue.
- Alongside the scenario anchors, you identify a set of tension fields. These are further drivers, or central dimensions, each expressed as a spectrum, or slider, between plausible opposing directions of development. Determined by how the scenario unfolds around its anchor, each scenario then sits at a different point on each slider. Because the same tension fields run across every scenario, you can read the whole set at a glance and see how each dimension plays out differently from one future to the next.

## Use scenario development to

- Provoke decision-makers to think differently about alternative future outcomes that may be difficult to grasp in the present.
- Create a basis for stress-testing strategies against plausible futures, and guide and inform organisations about the threats and opportunities those futures hold.
- Facilitate futures-oriented conversation that builds shared understanding and stimulates strategic thinking about the future.

## Key things to consider

- Scenarios are not predictions of the future. They are not meant to be 'right' or 'wrong', 'good' or 'bad'. Rather, their function is to challenge assumptions and explore alternative ways that the future may develop. Explorative scenarios can have a richness that quantitative methods can't capture, which also helps to stimulate creativity and to break from the conventional wisdom around present and short-term problems.
- Crafting credible and useful scenarios is difficult. A good scenario is not just an abstract analysis of trends. Rather, it tells a believable story set in the future. Using a 'behind-the-scenes' scenario grid to map out key variables and elements across all scenarios helps ensure consistency. Additionally, following general guidelines can support the writing of quality scenario narratives.
- To make a scenario 'come alive', it can be helpful to think about what different personas might be doing in the future. How are they living? What do they value? How are they impacted in this scenario?
- For scenarios to be used effectively in a decision-making process, stakeholders must be convinced of the soundness, relevance and value of the process. This hinges on the foundations on which scenarios are built, the structures that they use, and the reasoning they employ, which must stand up to critical examination.
- The articulation and presentation of scenarios depend greatly upon the intended users. Some scenarios stay at the level of broad generalities without much supporting analysis, which may make them less operational and less useful for decision-making, while they may still provide the general public with inspiration on potential futures. Other scenarios that are presented in much more technical and formalised ways may offer deeper insights for decision-making but can be challenging for ordinary readers to assimilate.
- In a decision-making context, experience suggests that when stakeholders evaluate scenarios that are too extreme or implausible, they often become more confident in their original assumptions about the future. On the other hand, scenarios that are not challenging enough are easily dismissed as pointless. Both situations can lead to distrust in the foresight process. Finding the right balance is crucial for scenarios to have an impact on decision-making.

## How to frame good scenario axes

Characteristics of good axes:

- Anchored in critical uncertainty: each axis should represent high impact and high uncertainty, the top right corner of the impact/uncertainty matrix.
- Explanatory power and relevance: strong explanatory value for the core area of interest and relevance for generating strategic insights. An axis should explain why the future might evolve in different ways.
- Stretch current thinking: able to challenge organisational perspectives and assumptions and provoke new thinking.
- Distinct, balanced polarities: each axis's polarities should describe clearly different logics or states of the world and represent balanced plausibility, both feeling credible with some evidence pointing toward them. Ideally 50/50, perhaps 60/40; beyond that it is no longer an uncertainty.
- Appropriate level of abstraction: an axis often combines several related uncertainties into a single, more meaningful dimension, allowing adequate exploration space and narrative richness.

Common pitfalls:

- Axis dependence: the two axes and their polarities must be independent of each other, or the axes 'collapse' into one dimension. Movement along one axis should not mean movement along the other, like two independent dials on a control panel. Public trust and political stability, or severity of climate impact and strength of climate policy, are pairs that collapse; demographic structure and geopolitical competition is a pair that holds.
- Normative framing: avoid framing polarities as 'good' versus 'bad', or likely versus unlikely. Keep them neutral and descriptive.
- One-to-one translation from the matrix: the impact/uncertainty matrix shows where critical uncertainty is present, but these uncertainties often need to be adjusted or reframed into broader logics to define scenario boundaries.
- Choosing two trends instead of two uncertainties, such as two developments that might both simply increase; one axis dominating the other, yielding two meaningful quadrants and two empty ones; conceptually interesting but strategically irrelevant axes that do not inform real-world choices.

Do not lock into axes too early. Experiment with different combinations on paper, and prompt with: do the four quadrants make intuitive and strategic sense? Can four distinct, believable futures be built around them? Adding a few bullets to each quadrant makes this clearer.

## Types of scenario approach

- 2 x 2 scenarios: built around two critical uncertainties that are independent and high impact, creating four structurally distinct worlds to stretch strategic thinking.
- Scenario archetypes: pre-imposed narrative structures such as growth, decline, constraint and transformation. Helpful for rapid framing, less tailored to specific system dynamics.
- Driver-based scenarios: organised around selected high-impact driving forces and assumptions about how they evolve, focusing on causal pathways rather than fixed narrative quadrants.
- Techniques from design disciplines: speculative and critical design methods that make futures tangible and experiential, prioritising provocation, imagination and embodied exploration over structural completeness.

## The scenario grid

The grid is the scaffolding behind scenario building. Its rows are the dimensions every scenario must address; its columns are the scenarios.

- Contextual dimensions are common to any scope: the zeitgeist or mood of the times, economy and global aspects, society and people, technology and the digital domain, sustainability and resources, regulation and governance.
- Context-specific dimensions belong to the scope in focus: for example the physical places and communities concerned, the landscape of the domain itself, the needs and expectations of its users, and work life.
- Scenarios should cover all three layers of the environment: megatrends and the contextual environment, the operating environment, and the strategy development space.

## Building a scenario

1. Spend a few minutes in silence listing the variables and dimensions that should be examined across all scenarios.
2. Start with the broad strokes: discuss the polarities that define the scenario and what their combination entails, prompting with "This is a world where...".
3. Work through the grid to unfold the scenario's dimensions, getting the dynamics right and making sure characteristics and events are plausible and consistent.
4. Build a short narrative from the grid that captures the essence of the scenario and helps people understand and relate to this future, with a catchy scenario title.
5. Present each scenario with its title, its narrative and an emerging dilemma. The scenario is not yet the organisation's strategy; it is a plausible future that should inform it.
