# Pre-mortem

## What it is

A pre-mortem is a short group exercise run at the start of a plan, project or strategy. The facilitator tells the group to assume the plan has been carried out and has failed badly, and asks each person to write down what caused the failure. The exercise inverts the post-mortem, which examines a failure after it has happened, and applies the same diagnostic attention before anything has gone wrong.

The technique rests on prospective hindsight. When people are told an outcome has already occurred and asked to explain it, they produce more specific and more accurate reasons than when asked what might go wrong. The certainty of the framing, however fictional, unlocks explanations that a hypothetical framing leaves buried.

## When to use it

- Once a plan is concrete enough to be briefed to the people who will carry it out, and before commitments make it costly to change.
- When a team has converged quickly on a plan and no one has voiced doubts.
- When the people most likely to see a weakness are junior, new, or otherwise reluctant to contradict the plan's owners in a normal review.
- In foresight work, inside each scenario in turn, so the same plan is made to fail under several different futures and the causes can be compared.

## The steps

1. Brief the group on the plan so everyone is working from the same picture.
2. Announce that the plan has been executed and has failed completely. State it as fact, not as a possibility.
3. Give everyone a few minutes to write, on their own and in silence, every reason for the failure they can think of. Encourage the reasons that would feel impolite or disloyal to raise in a normal meeting.
4. Go round the room. Each person reads one reason from their list, starting with the person who owns the plan. Continue until every distinct reason has been recorded. No reason is debated during the round.
5. After the session, the plan's owner works through the list and decides which reasons warrant changes to the plan, which need monitoring, and which can be set aside.

## What it protects against

- Silence in the planning phase. Reservations that would never be raised as objections come out readily as explanations of a failure that has supposedly already happened.
- Overinvestment. People who are committed to a plan tend to dismiss risks. Asking them to explain a failure loosens that grip without asking them to argue against their own work.
- Undervalued knowledge. Team members who name a weakness no one else saw are recognised for their experience, and the rest of the group learns what they knew.
- Late detection. Having rehearsed the ways the plan could fail, the team recognises the early signs of those failures sooner once the plan is under way.

## Exercise sheet: What would make this fail?

One of the kit's optional exercises: a one-page canvas with short instructions and space to work, for use with pen, paper and conversation, or alongside the assistant.

- Start with: one plan and a future review date.
- The canvas: a timeline moves from today's plan to imagined failure at a future date, then returns the learning to today. Three rows connect possible causes with changes or checks and responsibility.
- At the end: possible reasons for failure and practical changes or checks to make now.

On the sheet:

- After the briefing, agree what failure would mean at the future review date, then imagine that it has happened.
- Write alone for five minutes, in the past tense: "It failed because...".
- Go round the room, one reason per person per turn, until all are recorded. Discuss afterwards, and note a level of concern from 1 to 5 for each reason.
- Choose the most consequential concerns. For each, record what you can change or check, connected to its cause, with an owner and a review date.
