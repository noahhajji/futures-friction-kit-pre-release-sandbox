# Wind-tunnelling

Wind-tunnelling is an approach used to stress-test strategies or policies against a set of scenarios to evaluate how well they hold up under varying future conditions. It involves assessing the performance of different strategic options and objectives when facing given scenarios, considering the emerging issues and different future conditions that each scenario presents.

## How it works

- The exercise helps organisations understand which aspects of their strategy are resilient and which are vulnerable across different future contexts. It also helps identify specific aspects that may become important if a certain scenario begins to unfold.
- The outcome of a wind-tunnelling exercise is typically a matrix that assesses the effectiveness of strategic options and objectives across a range of scenarios.
- For each option, you assess whether it works and will have a positive impact, needs adaptation to be more effective, or whether it has no impact or may even lead to negative outcomes.

## Use wind-tunnelling to

- Explore how different future conditions might impact current strategies, identify the most resilient aspects, and become aware of weak spots.
- Evaluate strategic options and objectives against a range of plausible futures to see which perform well across them.
- Build a portfolio of options suited to different external conditions with the goal of balancing the need to define a clear strategy with the need to maintain a high level of strategic agility in uncertain conditions.

## Key things to consider

- Strategic actions or options that yield benefits in all or most scenarios are considered robust. These should be pursued as no-regret moves if resources allow. Actions that work disproportionately well in specific scenarios can serve as contingency plans to be adopted in suitable circumstances or modified to make them more robust across scenarios. Additionally, high-risk, high-reward 'big bets' could lead to significant breakthroughs or catastrophic failures depending on how the future unfolds. Taken together, these categories help you build a portfolio of actions that balances immediate needs with the potential need for quick pivots as the future unfolds.
- Wind-tunnelling can also be used the other way around. Rather than testing what you already have, you put existing strategies to one side and use each future as a resource for imagination. What solutions does this future point to? What different or altogether new strategies does it suggest?
- A variation of stress-testing worth trying is the pre-mortem. You start by establishing a strategy, priority or project, and then imagine it has failed in a given future. You then work backwards, speculating and debating the causes of that failure, to ultimately identify actions that would make the strategy more adaptive and resilient. Run the pre-mortem inside each scenario in turn, so the same strategy meets failure under different future conditions. The causes will differ from one scenario to the next, and comparing them shows not just whether a strategy is vulnerable but how it breaks in each world.
- The true value of wind-tunnelling comes from thinking through the consequences of an uncertain future and asking the question "What if this future happens?" Engaging a diverse set of stakeholders ensures a broader range of perspectives and helps identify strategic blind spots.

## Scenario-tested and scenario-led strategy

- Most organisations already have a strategy and a portfolio of initiatives and responses. The strategy-forward approach evaluates current strategies, initiatives, products, services, projects and risk responses against the set of scenarios, to see whether they still provide a path to success. This is wind-tunnelling.
- The future-back approach puts existing strategies to one side and starts from the scenarios, using each future as a resource for imagination: what solutions does this future indicate, what different or altogether new strategies does it suggest? In practice the two approaches are often blended.

## Decisions for robustness

- No-regret moves largely deliver benefits across all scenarios, regardless of how the future unfolds. These are typically implemented immediately.
- Options are largely geared toward offering benefits in some, but not all, scenarios. They can be scaled up or down and adjusted as conditions evolve, enabling faster pivots from the current strategy to another that has been pre-developed.
- Big bets are bold, high-stakes decisions that rely on strong convictions about specific aspects of the future. They often require significant commitment and involve limited reversibility, so they should be selectively implemented, as they may stake the organisation's future.
- Strategic options are the courses of action an organisation can consider; strategic objectives are the specific goals it aims to achieve within a defined timeframe. Options can be deployed at any point of the value chain, not only in customer-facing products and services: optimising logistics networks, partnerships, joint ventures and acquisitions, procurement and sourcing, divesting or leaving markets, new revenue streams.

## Running it in a room

- Each group holds one scenario and has developed initiatives from within it. A 'host' stays with the group's initiatives to explain them and answer questions; the rest rotate around the room, visiting each of the other scenarios in turn.
- From your own scenario's perspective, assess whether each other group's initiatives would work and add value in your world. Mark green where the initiative is robust, yellow where it could work with modifications, red where it would not work, would be counter-productive or would destroy value.
- Debrief in the group and in plenary: do any initiatives prove robust across all scenarios? Did any seem particularly attractive, worth a bet? Could several be pursued at once, for the value of strategic options? Could elements of several be combined into one superior strategy?

## Exercise sheet: Would this idea survive a different future?

One of the kit's optional exercises: a one-page canvas with short instructions and space to work, for use with pen, paper and conversation, or alongside the assistant.

- Start with: up to three options or strategic initiatives and a set of plausible scenarios for the same time horizon. Agree the aim and time horizon, and test every option against the same scenarios.
- The canvas: a matrix with up to three options as rows and four scenarios as columns. Each cell holds an impact judgement and a short reason.
- At the end: an options-by-scenarios matrix showing what works, what needs adapting and what could fail.

On the sheet:

- Add a judgement and a reason in every cell: + works, ~ needs adaptation, 0 no impact, - negative impact, ? unclear.
- Read across the matrix: which options hold up across scenarios, and which depend on particular conditions? Keep disagreements and unknowns visible.
- Scenarios are plausible alternatives, not predictions. If you only have a single trend, develop contrasting future conditions before filling the matrix.
