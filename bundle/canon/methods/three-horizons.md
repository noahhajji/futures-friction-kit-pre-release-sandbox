# Three horizons

The Three Horizons framework, developed by Bill Sharpe, is useful to assess pathways of change, and how change can unfold over different time horizons. The framework assumes that change happens in waves in which a dominant form is eventually overtaken and displaced by another. Emerging change will challenge our current paradigm and assumptions, and over time today's decisions, policies and products will become obsolete. This is particularly useful for understanding societal transitions, where a status quo or dominant system declines and a new system rises in its place.

## The three horizons

- Horizon 1 (H1) is the dominant system at present. It represents the 'business as usual' in terms of developments and current assumptions that are important/manifested in the present and that define how we engage with the world. We rely on these systems being stable and reliable. But as the world changes, aspects of 'business as usual' begin to feel out of place, misaligned or even obsolete.
- Eventually, H1 will be superseded by new systems, developments and realisations that will grow in importance in the medium term: Horizon 2 (H2). Exactly how H2 will develop may not be apparent yet. Some aspects will be absorbed into the H1 systems to improve them and to prolong their life, while others will facilitate a transition from the present to Horizon 3.
- Horizon 3 (H3) will bring transformational shifts and completely new paradigms from the present.

## Order of exploration

- You normally progress through the exploration of the different horizons in this order: H1, H3, H2.
- Without establishing H3 before H2, it becomes impossible to make the distinction between aspects in H2 that will contribute to sustaining H1 and those that will facilitate a transition to H3.
- This sequence will also help prevent 'path-dependent' thinking through the three horizons.

## Use Three Horizons to

- Reveal current assumptions about the future and make them explicit, allowing us to reimagine how transformational shifts away from today may arrive.
- Better assess potential pathways of change, and identify drivers that maintain the status quo and drivers that push towards transition.
- Recognise how strategies aligned with the present will decline in changing and emerging conditions, and where current assumptions leave you vulnerable.
- Highlight the value of thinking in different horizons at the same time.

## Key things to consider

- The Three Horizons exercise should ideally be highly collaborative and include diverse perspectives. This is key to revealing current assumptions and making them explicit, better allowing us to reimagine how we might arrive at a Horizon 3 future that represents transformational shifts from today.
- Remember to debrief the key takeaways from the exercise, with a specific focus on the most interesting and intriguing Horizon 3 futures and the most relevant 'transitions' in Horizon 2.
- The three horizons should not just be seen as abstract representations of change, but as three qualitatively different orientations to the future in the present. There is no fixed definition of what 'near- to mid- to long term' means and all three horizons are always present. For example, early manifestations of H3, pockets of the future, are already visible in the present.
- A powerful feature of the framework is the built-in distinction between aspects that are maintaining or improving on the status quo versus those that are pushing towards transformation. This helps organisations think about change that has the potential for deep transformation. The displacement of horizons may be gradual, but in times of rapid change they can be quite abrupt as different tipping points may occur.

## Strategic fit changes over time

- The vertical axis of the framework is the degree of strategic fit with the external environment. The first horizon, the prevailing system as it continues into the future, loses fit over time as its environment changes. The third horizon holds ideas or arguments about the future of the system that are at best marginal in the present, 'pockets of future' embedded in it, but which over time may displace the first. The second horizon is the intermediate space where the first and third collide, a space of transition that is typically unstable.
- Two kinds of thinking sit over the chart. Tactical thinking, working from data and evidence, is the traditional strategist's 'predict and control', improving the existing. Transformation and novelty, explorative and creative, is the work of strategic foresight, challenging assumptions and anticipating novelty. Which kind fits depends on where the organisation stands on the horizons.

## Exercise sheet: Where does this innovation belong?

One of the kit's optional exercises: a one-page canvas with short instructions and space to work, for use with pen, paper and conversation, or alongside the assistant.

- Start with: three to five innovation projects and a short description of each.
- The canvas: three curves on a time axis, where H1 declines, H2 rises and falls and H3 rises, with writing areas that explore H1, H3 and the H2 transition.
- At the end: a map of where each innovation project belongs, the reasons behind it and one missing connection.

The people doing the exercise place each innovation project by the change it supports, and write why. A project's purpose and effects determine the placement, never its launch date or its technology. Three questions guide it, taken in the order of exploration, H1, H3, H2:

1. H1: does it fit the current strategy? Are you optimising existing products and processes based on data, control and return on investment in the short term?
2. H3: is it something that completely rethinks? Is it a pocket of future that serves a new ecosystem? Is it challenging core assumptions?
3. H2: is it an experiment that might soon fit? Is your project a smaller experiment that might fit for a short time or be a bridge towards H3?
4. One missing connection: look at the map as a whole. Which connection is missing between what you do today and the H3 you have described?
