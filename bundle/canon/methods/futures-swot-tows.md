# Futures SWOT-TOWS

TOWS is derived from the SWOT analysis and is a practical tool to develop strategic options from an external and internal analysis, by matching current internal factors to future external factors. In strategic foresight it is used to identify relationships between an organisation's current strengths and weaknesses and the opportunities and threats in alternative futures, so as to identify the strategic options the organisation could pursue.

## How it works

The matrix crosses the internal present with the external future.

| Internal, present / External, future | Opportunities (O) | Threats (T) |
|---|---|---|
| Strengths (S) | S-O strategies: how can you leverage your strengths to capitalise on future opportunities? | S-T strategies: how can you leverage your strengths to avoid or minimise the impact of future threats? |
| Weaknesses (W) | W-O strategies: how can you address your weaknesses to capitalise on future opportunities? | W-T strategies: how can you address your weaknesses to reduce the impact of future threats? |

- For one scenario at a time, reflect on the opportunities and threats that emerge in it and on the organisation's current strengths and weaknesses that might influence how it can respond.
- Identify two to four strategic initiatives the organisation could implement in response to the strategic implications and the TOWS analysis, and capture each on its own initiative template.
- Be as clear as possible in the strategic reasoning and keep initiatives as actionable as possible. Avoid all-encompassing initiatives that try to capture everything believed to be important. Several options can apply to the same future opportunity or threat.

## Key things to consider

- S-O strategies are often the easiest and most intuitive to work with, because future opportunities are often maximised through innovation of new products and services that fit future needs, or business model innovation to fit a future competitive landscape. Those more versed in risk management may find the threats side easier.
- Initiatives tend to land at the customer end of the value chain. Organisations deploy strategic options at every point of the value chain, not only in customer-facing product and service development: optimising logistics networks, partnerships, joint ventures and acquisitions, procurement and sourcing, divesting or leaving markets, new revenue streams.
- The initiatives are the input to wind-tunnelling, where they are tested across the whole scenario set.
