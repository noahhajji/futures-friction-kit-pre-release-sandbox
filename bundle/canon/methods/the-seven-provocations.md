# The Seven Provocations

The Seven Provocations, first developed in-house at CIFS, uses structured provocation to surface the assumptions underpinning a current view of the future, confront them, and explore what follows if the future behaves differently. The assumptions it targets are the embedded beliefs that underpin an organisation's understanding of how the future will behave, and ultimately its future strategic outlook. These are assumptions that often go unexamined in strategic thinking, and, left unchallenged, they can create a confirmation pathway towards a flawed or overly deterministic view of the future.

The method is simple and accessible by design. While provocation is embedded in many traditional foresight methods, the Seven Provocations is particularly valuable in contexts where those methods may prove too abstract, complex or demanding.

## How it works

1. An assumption audit surfaces the assumptions central to your understanding of how the future will unfold, making explicit the external conditions that need to hold true for a current outlook to be 'on point'.
2. You reframe those assumptions through the lens of one or more of the seven provocations.
3. You play out the implications if the provoked version of the future were to unfold.

## The seven provocations

Each of the seven provocations takes a different angle, depending on the nature of the assumption you are seeking to challenge.

### Provocations that challenge assumptions about direction, continuity and inevitability

- The Flip: What if an assumption you hold turns out to be misaligned, or even in direct opposition, to how the future eventually unfolds?
- The Discontinuity: What if defining features of current reality or dominant 'ways of doing' are rendered obsolete in the future?
- The Resistant: What if a development or change you strongly expect to happen is delayed or stopped by the weight of present structures or habits?

### Provocations that challenge assumptions about timing and pace

- The Overcorrection: What if an emerging change that appears transformative today levels off or becomes less transformative in the long term?
- The Undercorrection: What if a change that seems marginal or easy to dismiss today becomes far more transformative in the longer run than initially assumed?

### Provocations that challenge emotionally and morally loaded assumptions

- The Uncomfortable Truth: Which uncomfortable or inconvenient 'truths' about the future are being consciously or subconsciously avoided?
- The Value Lock-in: As we lock in on a transformation towards a system or paradigm seen as more desirable, what embedded constraints, exclusions or dependencies might shape that preferred future in limiting ways?

## Use the Seven Provocations to

- Surface the assumptions underpinning an organisation's current outlook on the future, and by extension the strategic decisions, business models and policies that stem from it.
- Work through what would follow, strategically, if a foundational assumption does not hold and the future behaves differently from what is expected.
- Bring provocation into a strategic conversation on the future where more demanding foresight methods would be too abstract, complex or resource-heavy.

## Key things to consider

- Some assumptions are explicit and widely shared across organisations or industries, while others are more implicit and specific to a particular context. By asking people to articulate why they believe something to be true, their assumptions become embedded in the answers. The facilitator's role is then to listen critically, identify those assumptions, both explicit and implicit, and make them discussable.
- The provocations can be used individually or in combination. While some provocations may overlap and some implications will repeat, each offers a distinct lens to reframe what is currently taken for granted about the future. When selecting which provocations to use, it is helpful to consider the nature of the assumptions you are seeking to challenge.
- The Value Lock-in is the most philosophically layered of the seven and may be less operationally relevant at an organisational level than the others. But it is also the one that provokes the deepest layer of assumptions.
- The template provides a practical structure for working through one provocation at a time. You can use it repeatedly to test different assumptions, or to apply several provocations to the same one. Remember to capture additional reflections on whether the provocation exposes something worth exploring further.

## Figure: the Seven Provocations template

A blank worksheet. A single field runs across the top, provocation used, followed by a fill-in line. Below it are four empty columns for working through one provocation: assumption to challenge (state an assumption, expectation or belief about how the future will unfold), reframed assumption (rewrite the assumption through the lens of the provocation), potential (strategic) implications (list key implications if this provoked version of the future were to unfold), and additional reflections (consider how central this assumption is to your current outlook, and whether this provocation exposes something worth exploring further).

## Exercise sheet: What are we assuming?

One of the kit's optional exercises: a one-page canvas with short instructions and space to work, for use with pen, paper and conversation, or alongside the assistant.

- Start with: one plan and the period it needs to work in. Name the plan and its time horizon, then choose one assumption in the plan: what do we expect to be true about how the future will unfold?
- The canvas: the seven provocations as prompts beside four writing columns for an assumption, its reframing, implications and reflections.
- At the end: one assumption reframed, its implications and a question worth exploring.
