# Signposting

Signposting is a structured approach to monitoring change in the external environment by identifying and tracking specific developments that indicate how key drivers are evolving, or whether future scenarios are beginning to unfold. Where most foresight tools help you explore what could happen, signposting helps you notice what is actually happening, and when it might be time to respond.

## How it works

- Signposting builds directly on previous foresight work, often Driver Mapping or Scenario Development, and is set up accordingly.
- Progressing from Driver Mapping, signposting monitors how key drivers evolve over time.
- Progressing from Scenario Development, signposts are tied to the critical uncertainties or key characteristics within each scenario, so that developments can be tracked to assess whether a particular scenario, or aspects of it, is starting to take shape.
- The value of signposting lies in timing. Foresight work widens the range of futures an organisation is prepared for, but it cannot say which one is arriving.
- The signposts should be defined as observable indicators that give early indication of which way things are moving, while triggers are specific thresholds or events that, once reached, signal that action or adjustment is needed.
- Together, they translate abstract scenarios into measurable realities, and a reached threshold becomes the prompt to revisit a strategic priority, activate a contingency plan or shift resources.

## Use signposting to

- Assess whether a particular scenario is beginning to play out, or whether a key driver is accelerating, decelerating or transforming.
- Establish when to act, by defining in advance what would signal that a strategic adjustment is needed.
- Keep foresight work alive after a project ends, translating scenarios into something that can be checked against reality and discussed over time.

## Key things to consider

- A good signpost is specific and observable, rather than a vague direction of development. It has to be something from which you can actually derive a shift in the relevant driver or scenario. Avoid indicators like 'regulatory changes' and focus on something you could point at and agree had happened, such as 'new privacy protection legislation being announced in three or more major jurisdictions'. That said, a signpost does not have to be a number. Some developments can be measured and supported by data, while others are only partially quantifiable and better understood through examples and observation.
- Keep the list focused. A handful of well-chosen signposts is more effective than a long list that dilutes attention and becomes impossible to maintain. For each one, decide what specifically will be monitored, how often it will be reviewed, and what would count as a significant development.
- Review and refine the signposts periodically. Because they are derived from a particular reading of drivers and scenarios, they go stale when that reading changes. Remove those that have become irrelevant and add new ones as strategic priorities shift, new intelligence emerges, or the underlying foresight work is revisited.
- Be cautious of confirmation bias. It is easy to notice the developments that confirm what you already believe, or hope, and to overlook equally valid signals pointing the other way. Aim for balanced evaluation by deliberately looking for evidence of outcomes you would rather not see.

The first three columns, signpost (the observable development clearly described), driver/scenario (what this signpost tells you about) and threshold/trigger (what counts as significant), are marked as clearly defined when setting up.
