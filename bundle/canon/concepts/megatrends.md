# Megatrends

Long-term and large-scale transformative forces that profoundly shape both the present and the future.

- For the most part, megatrends stay their course even in turbulent times.
- They shape the future landscape of societies, economies, industries and cultures over extended periods.
- While they are global in scope, their impact varies locally.
- In a principally uncertain and complex world, megatrends help us recognise that there are also relatively stable, yet powerful, forces at work in shaping future outcomes.

## CIFS definition

Megatrends are durable, directional structural forces that reshape the conditions under which societies, individuals and organisations across the world operate. They are historically specific rather than permanent features of human societies, resilient to individual action and short-term disruption, and grounded in dynamics strong enough to persist for decades.

## Historical, durable and non-linear forces

- Megatrends are historically situated rather than transhistorical forces. Transhistorical forces are permanent features of human societies, such as demographic change or technological development, which are sometimes considered megatrends. Some megatrends can be understood as historically specific configurations of such processes, the Inversion of the Population Pyramid as a configuration of demographic change, or the Rise of Embedded AI as a configuration of technological development, but megatrends do not persist indefinitely. They emerge from a specific configuration of conditions (that could in principle be different), unfold through distinct mechanisms, and have systemic and directional effects that run their course over decades.
- Technological development and demographic change do not exhibit these properties. They are features of every human society, take different forms across time and place, and do not depend on any specific configuration of conditions. Treating them as megatrends would be like treating 'social change' or 'conflict' as megatrends: too broad to be analytically useful and too permanent to tell us much about what makes the conditions of change in the present and immediate future distinct.
- Megatrends do not necessarily unfold in a linear fashion. Even though they persist over the long term, they can suffer temporary interruptions, and a disruption may last several years without spelling the end of the megatrend. The Green Transition has faced several interruptions, including energy crises pushing some countries back toward fossil fuels in the short run, or powerful political leaders working against green energy. These disruptions may have temporarily slowed the trajectory but have not ended it. The structural conditions and imperatives that necessitate a green energy transition in the long term are too powerful to be counteracted by such short-term disruptions.

## Typology of representation

- Megatrends, and all trends, can be represented in various ways, owing in part to differences in the kind of evidence that can substantiate them. Some are primarily supported by measurable developments and data that can be tracked over time, while others are primarily explained through observation and interpretation that cannot easily be measured in quantitative terms, often the case with megatrends relating to systems, values and social practices. Every megatrend always carries elements of both.
- The simplest form of representation is growth or decline trends, tracking a continuous increase or decrease in a set of measurable variables, such as the growth in urban populations as a share of the global total. Growth or decline can also be represented qualitatively, where a phenomenon is recognised to be increasing or decreasing in strength or significance even though the evidence is too qualitative to be measured precisely.
- Other trends are represented as transitions or shifts from one state to another, in which the elements of a system are rearranged into a new pattern, such as the shift from a unipolar to a multipolar world order. Shifts like this can rest on both quantitative and qualitative evidence.

## Megatrend diagnostics

CIFS has developed a diagnostics tool that tests a megatrend candidate against the definition's criteria, so that the process of megatrend selection rests on a shared, transparent and repeatable standard. The tool helps conceptually distinguish megatrends as higher-order, structurally significant transformations from shorter-term developments, transhistorical processes, or overly diffuse descriptions of change. This is consistent with CIFS' treatment of megatrends as analytically bounded aggregate patterns that help frame the long-term contextual environment rather than explain specific outcomes on their own.

Each criterion functions as a diagnostic gate. A 'No' at any stage indicates that the phenomenon does not qualify as a megatrend candidate under the CIFS definition. Although the tool may seem to encourage binary yes/no assessments, each gate calls for interpretation and judgement. The definition does not point to one definitive set of megatrends, and the eleven trends defined by CIFS are not necessarily an exhaustive list. One organisation applying the same criteria might draw boundaries or weigh evidence differently, group developments in another way, or be motivated by different domain expertise. This is not a weakness of the approach but a product of the nature of megatrends as abstractions that we try to represent analytically.

### Figure: CIFS megatrend diagnostics

A vertical flowchart of seven numbered gates. Each gate is a box containing a question and its explanation. A 'yes' below each box passes the candidate down to the next gate; a box to the right gives the 'no' outcome that disqualifies it. Passing all seven gates qualifies the candidate as a megatrend under the CIFS definition.

| Gate | Test | If no |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Can we define what is changing? | A megatrend candidate must have clearly defined boundaries: what it includes, what it excludes, and what evidence would count as support. 'Acceleration' and 'systemic complexity' are sometimes named as megatrends, but neither passes this gate. Both describe properties of change itself rather than a domain within which change occurs. | Not analytically clear |
| 2. Is there a clear directional pattern? | The direction of a megatrend must be clearer than mere 'change', though it may follow different patterns: growth or decline, transition or shift, and others. | No clear direction |
| 3. Will it have transformative societal impact? | A megatrend must reshape the conditions under which societies, people and organisations operate, not merely be something big, persistent or widespread. | Not transformative |
| 4. Is it grounded in dynamics likely to persist for decades? | Many things feel important and durable in the moment, to the extent that they are mistaken for deeper structural change. A megatrend must be grounded in dynamics that have built up over years and are robust enough to be sustained over decades. | Not structurally durable |
| 5. Is it historically situated, not transhistorical? | A megatrend must be rooted in a specific configuration of society rather than being a permanent feature of it. Demographic change is transhistorical, since it has always happened. Population ageing, by contrast, is historically situated: a distinct demographic trajectory produced by present conditions. | Too permanent |
| 6. Is its momentum resilient to individual action and short-term disruption? | All megatrends are contingent on human action. While they can suffer temporary interruptions, they cannot be easily reversed by single actions or events, unless those events are as rare as they are transformative in scope and impact. | Too contingent |
| 7. Is the evidence base defensible? | A megatrend must be supported by evidence from a diverse range of sources. The underlying body of evidence must be coherent and not selectively chosen to fit a conclusion. | Not sufficiently supported |

## Origin and other definitions

- The term was coined by John Naisbitt in 1982. Trends, like horses, are easier to ride in the direction they are going: if we know where the horse is heading, we can position ourselves to ride with the change rather than be overtaken or run over by it. Megatrends are less about prediction and more about awareness of the direction of travel.
- The European Commission defines a megatrend as a long-term driving force that is observable now and will continue to have a global impact in years to come.
- In a principally unpredictable world, megatrends stand out as the only relative certainty we have and give points of orientation for the longer term. Their trajectory and velocity are not set in stone, and which megatrends jump out depends on the country and sector in focus. You cannot hide from a megatrend; the only real choice is how to respond.

## A checklist and a hierarchy

- Is this a megatrend? Multidisciplinary rather than narrow; a driver of change with transformative power; breadth, meaning a far-reaching, global effect; a long timespan of ten to fifteen years; forward focused, defining the future world.
- Megatrends sit on top of a complex weave of trends. Sub-trends are observable or measurable directional shifts with narrower scope or impact, possibly shorter lived, which may even run counter to a megatrend. Weak signals are subtle, often early indicators of emerging shifts that are not yet widely recognised, with the potential to significantly impact the future but which may also fade away. There is no single correct way to group trends; different organisations and futurists cluster them differently.
- Megatrends are not independent of one another. They interact, overlap and reinforce: demographic developments affect technology, economic models and regulation, and technological breakthroughs reshape economic models and societies. The ripple effect is strong.
