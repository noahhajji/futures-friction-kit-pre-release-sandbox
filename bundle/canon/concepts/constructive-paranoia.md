# Constructive paranoia and no-regret moves

Many venerable news organisations will, in all likelihood, die an undignified death, and many talented journalists may no longer be needed in an AI-mediated reality. The future of journalism is nevertheless not predetermined. Gard Steiro's image is the Titanic's bandleader: a proud institution, shaped by a strong culture that has served it well and cemented its working methods, has hit an iceberg whose extent beneath the surface nobody knows, and keeps playing. A ship that size cannot change course in a second.

## Constructive paranoia

- The most dangerous thing a media leader can do is downplay the challenges. Genuine understanding of the need for change in a legacy organisation requires brutal honesty about the weaknesses of today's journalism and products.
- Steiro trusts people who are genuinely uncertain and dare to admit it over self-appointed oracles; uncertainty builds trust in organisations of thinking people.
- The culture to aim for: always alert, never taking users' trust for granted, always looking for ways to improve, incrementally and radically, and never paralysed by uncertainty.

## No-regret moves

In a landscape with no map, look for the moves that will not be regretted whatever comes.

1. Understand what users expect. Too many newsrooms still fail to navigate by signals from their audiences. The era when audiences came simply to receive information they had neither asked for nor needed is ending; we are moving from attention to intention, and a strategy of making the products we think brilliant and hoping audiences will understand will fail. Quantitative data, and meeting users face to face.
2. Structured journalism. The article is not sick; it is stone dead as the unit around which journalism is planned and produced. Journalists need to focus on the story, not the article, and prepare for a world in which much of the value lies in atoms of journalism: quotes, images, audio, verified facts and data, readable by machines as well as humans. Some form of liquid content, but in a way that preserves editorial, ethical and professional standards.
3. Culture eats everything. A new tool or CMS will not save the media; the ability to act quickly and a culture of innovation will. Hierarchical, top-down organisations will struggle. Invest in educating staff in AI, not to use tools but to apply the technology intelligently within their own domains, and give people with the will to rethink journalism the freedom to experiment.
4. Document every workflow. What actually happens in the newsroom, how journalism is produced and quality-assured, what the secret sauce is: surprisingly often undocumented. Without it the potential of AI cannot be realised and resources cannot be reprioritised. Schibsted mapped every editorial workflow across its brands, assessing time spent, tool support, AI potential and urgency, and so knows where capacity can be freed and where to focus AI to move resources towards unique journalism.
5. Automate as much as you can. Different organisations will choose different identities, some marketed as made by humans; all will benefit from automating wherever they can. Push towards the boundary, automate until it hurts a little, because only then are the possibilities understood, and only then is the most valuable resource freed: people.
6. Double down on what AI cannot replace. Adopting AI is not the hard part; identifying the journalism one can make a living from after the shift is. It will involve the classical virtues: source networks, finding what someone is trying to hide, analysing complex issues, being out in the real world with the people served. This is about competence as much as time; there may be fewer journalists, but the best may have a brighter future than ever.
7. Trust, trust, trust. Trust will be the most important KPI. If the trust dimension is lost, convenience wins and people might as well ask a chatbot. Trust has many drivers: relevance, the willingness to correct mistakes, adherence to publicly understood ethical standards. Be more transparent about editorial decisions and dilemmas, and build ethical standards and quality control directly into the automated systems.
8. Launch speedboats. Loyal users are happy with existing products and sceptical of radical change; a potentially huge new audience expects a different experience; satisfying both at once may be impossible, and changing established products too fast loses the people paying for the transformation. VG's answer is speedboats: real products in the market, designed for the next generation of news consumers, where bold decisions can be taken without risking the trust of the main products' users. The learning is the point; what works is brought back to the mothership once there is evidence.
9. Don't get lost. When everything changes and visibility is poor, hold the compass. Journalism is about giving citizens the information they need to make informed choices, and about the people who depend on it to be heard, seen and taken seriously. That part is unlikely to be replaced by technology.

## Key things to consider

- The dream scenario is that the core mission survives: that some organisations emerge stronger, preserve their role as providers of verified information, and that audiences still see the value in someone travelling out into the world to observe and document on their behalf, with interfaces that are different, much of today's work automated, and agents visiting at least as often as humans.
- When the hull is leaking, the stupidest thing to do is sit still. Keep on playing, but find a new tune.
