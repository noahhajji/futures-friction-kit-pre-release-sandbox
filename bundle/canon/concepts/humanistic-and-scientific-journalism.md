# Humanistic and scientific journalism

The same division shows up again and again under different names: romantic and modernist journalism, the differentiated and the scale ends of the barbell effect, editorial and technical. David Caswell's terms for what lies beneath them are C. P. Snow's two cultures. The humanistic culture emphasises subjective experience, interpretation, judgement and the qualia of human experience; the scientific culture emphasises empirical observation, experimentation, quantification and the accumulation of objectively verifiable knowledge.

## The barbell effect

- When a scaling technology appears, an industry separates into two extremes: a differentiated extreme based on intangible value, and a scale extreme based on applying the technology to its full extent. Businesses in the middle range decline, like department stores in an era of boutique brands and e-commerce.
- AI has deeply unsettled the balance between the two cultures, because it encroaches on what was the sole preserve of humanism: it can research, create, write, interpret, contextualise and make assessments, and its output can be measured against that of humans.

## Humanistic journalism

- Most journalism today. It is defined by the centrality of the opinions of specific humans called journalists, editors and publishers: an artisanal craft, a handmade product shaped by the intuitive choices of individuals, whose practitioners define themselves through socialisation, identity and values.
- Opinion is everywhere in it, from commissioning and newsworthiness to editing and word choice. The best have a voice; the very best are adept at storytelling. It is intensely social: a person speaking to other people, with trust, reputation, status and norms attached, and it is adept at triggering emotion, empathy, outrage and, for the most skilled, curiosity.
- As a social activity, automating it may be pointless or meaningless. Its human origins may become more valuable in an AI-mediated ecosystem, not because of vaguely defined quality but precisely because it is human, as chess did after Deep Blue and Go after AlphaGo.

## Scientific journalism

- Today it exists only on the margins: data journalism, the numerical parts of sports and financial reporting, election results, investigations built on databases and document repositories, fringe innovation projects. It is not prestigious, and some journalists barely consider it journalism.
- It is defined by abstraction and independence from human opinion. It values precision and objectivity, seeks to be systematic rather than selective and to build systems rather than handcraft artefacts, treats every unit of information the same, and is assessed by completeness and accuracy. The practitioner's opinion in its output is an error, not an attribute. It is not seeking to be socially influential; it is seeking to be useful.
- It is eminently automatable, and its automation began before general-purpose language models, in template-based natural language generation. The earlier limit to structured or numerical information was a technical constraint, now removed.
- A scientific perspective also applies to journalism's understanding of its audience, which is remarkably thin: at worst paternalistic folk theories, at best simplistic analytics and coarse declared preferences. Science offers cognitive research, controlled testing and theory built on neuroscience and evolutionary biology rather than on identity and social conditions.

## Why society needs scientific journalism

- A technical interpretation of societal information flows is inevitable, because humanistic interpretations cannot adjust to the scale, scope and velocity of AI-mediated information. The seeds are sprouting in start-ups, platforms and academic fields with no overlap with journalism; the ambition to replace journalism with an entirely new model of information flows, backed by billions, is one harbinger.
- The problem with that inevitable alternative is not its existence or value but its values. Journalism's declared values of truthfulness and independence, and its watchdog incentives, have served society well. A scientific journalism should not merely operate in a scientific way but pursue values similar to those of science itself, and ideally the enlightenment values of humanistic journalism should become its values too.
- Science is trusted because it works, and because it is not perceived to be filtered through the gate of human opinion. Humanistic journalism excels at inter-subjective truth, what a community agrees is true; scientific journalism may offer society a way to coalesce around a shared perception of objective reality that opinion-centred journalism cannot.

## What follows

- Explicitly recognise and separate humanistic from scientific journalism, in leadership, skills, activities, products and even organisations, and transfer the values and mission of the former into the latter. The differences between the cultures are too great to bridge; separation relieves both of their uncertainty, refocusing humanistic journalism on what it does best and unblocking scientific journalism.
- Build a research and development ecosystem for scientific journalism: first-principles applied research on information representation, automated verification, network coordination and incentives; audience research with the full range of cognitive science; AI and data science oriented towards societal understanding rather than support for storytelling.
- Humanistic journalism cannot become an end in itself, privileged over the goal of open, democratic societies of empowered citizens with access to trustworthy information. Journalism must expand or it will shrink.
