# Berkana two-loop model

Margaret Wheatley and Deborah Frieze of the Berkana Institute describe how a living system changes as two loops. A dominant system rises, peaks and declines. While it declines, an emergent system begins at its margins, among people who have walked out of the dominant system to work differently, and grows until it becomes the next system of influence. The two loops overlap in time: the old system is still running while the new one forms.

## The dominant system

- Stewards keep the dominant system working well for as long as it is still needed.
- Stabilisers hold the system steady at its peak.
- Hospice workers help the dominant system die with dignity, and compost what it has learned so that the emergent system can draw on it.
- Bridge builders carry people, resources and legitimacy from the dominant system across the transition into the emergent one.

## The emergent system

- Pioneers, also called walk-outs, are the people who leave the dominant system, or work at its edge, to try something that the dominant system no longer provides.
- Networks form when pioneers find each other and share what they are doing.
- Communities of practice form when a network turns into deliberate, committed relationships in which members develop a shared practice and new knowledge.
- A system of influence has emerged when the practices of those communities become the accepted way of doing things.

## The four moves

The emergent system does not grow by itself. Those who want it to grow do four things, in order:

1. Naming: recognise the pioneers and name what they are doing, so that they and others can see it as the beginning of something.
2. Connecting: bring the pioneers into contact with each other, so that isolated efforts become a network.
3. Nourishing: give the network what it needs to become a community of practice, time, resources, attention and the chance to learn together.
4. Illuminating: make the new practice visible to the wider system, so that it can be seen, understood and taken up, and become a system of influence.

## Key things to consider

- Living systems do not scale by replicating a programme. They scale through emergence: separate local efforts become connected, and a system of influence appears from the connections, often suddenly.
- The pioneers are easy to overlook or dismiss from inside the dominant system, because what they do looks marginal by its measures. The CIFS reading of the model is that change agents on the edges need visibility and support, not marginalisation.
- Both loops need people. Stewards and hospice workers are as necessary as pioneers, and the transition fails without the bridge builders between them.

## Figure: the two loops

Two curves drawn against a dashed horizontal line. The upper curve, the dominant system, rises from the left, peaks and falls to the right, ending at a point marked death and compost. Along it sit the stewards on the rising side, the stabilisers at the peak and hospice on the falling side. The lower curve, the emergent system, begins below the line near the start of the dominant system's decline, dips, and rises steeply on the right to a point marked new system of influence. Its stages are numbered along the curve: 1 naming, 2 connecting, 3 nourishing, 4 illuminating. Pioneers are shown as scattered dots below the line at the start of the emergent curve, with an arrow to a cluster labelled networks, from which an arrow leads to a ring labelled community of practice, and from there an arrow up to the new system of influence. A dashed line labelled transition runs from hospice on the dominant curve to bridge builders near the top of the emergent one.
