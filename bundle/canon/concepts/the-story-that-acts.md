# The story that acts

Ma is a Japanese word for an interval: the pause, the space between things that gives them their shape, the rest between notes that turns sound into music, space held open on purpose. Ezra Eeman's question is whether AI could do that for journalism, giving back the space to think rather than crowding it out.

## From attention to intention

- Digital media organised itself around attention: measured, predicted, sold. Agents move the conversation towards intention: what is someone trying to achieve, what can we help them do. Answer engines give the answer, not the page; personal agents brief you in your preferred tone, length and topics.
- A system that serves intentions can still enclose. Especially when it starts anticipating them, deciding what you probably want before you have had the chance to think. Media that fits like a second skin: exciting, and slightly suffocating.
- Carried through, this gives a media environment that fits so closely there is little room left to move, every interest recognised, every hesitation interpreted, every gap filled. In machine learning that is overfitting: a model so tuned to its training data that it fails on new examples. Erik Hoel's hypothesis is that dreaming keeps the brain from overfitting by injecting noise. An overfitted media environment is one that knows you so well it can no longer surprise you.

## Making room for judgement

- Sometimes we have a poorly formed question, change our mind while answering it, or need something precisely because we had no idea it existed. You cannot ask for what you do not know you are missing. Journalism has always brought those things into view.
- This is not a plea to make information difficult. A person who needs a clear explanation should get one; a person struggling with language, time or access should not have to do extra work to qualify for journalism. There is plenty of friction worth removing. The question is where removing it helps someone and where it starts to take over the work of forming a judgement.
- A story might help compare two proposals without selecting the one it thinks you should prefer, explain what is known while leaving a disputed question visibly unresolved, or give a place to begin without arranging the rest of the journey. There is a difference between leaving someone lost and giving them room to find their own way.

## The story that acts

- A story that both has and gives agency. It tells you what happened and can also offer a service grounded in the reporting: help with a calculation, a comparison or a practical next step. Rather than a generic chat box under every article, it begins with what this piece of journalism has established and what someone might do with it.
- It should never make the reader less involved in deciding where to go. There must be room to refuse its suggestion, follow a different thread, or arrive at a conclusion the newsroom did not expect; otherwise it is a more capable product and a less capable relationship.
- Eeman's imagined example, a local newsroom in 2030 reporting on the redevelopment of an industrial site: alongside the article, an offer to explore the changes around a chosen street, compare the current plan with the previous version, or ask what evidence supports a traffic claim, with the answers showing where the newsroom's work runs out. While exploring the road to a school, the reader encounters reporting about residents who may be displaced, which the story makes visible because it matters to the public decision, not because it was asked for. The story can help prepare a consultation response, pointing out an unsupported assumption, without deciding the position. For weeks afterwards there is silence, because nothing has met the agreed threshold; when a revised proposal appears, an explanation of the difference arrives, and when the consultation closes, the offer closes too. Acting and leaving space belong together.
- Not every story needs to turn into a service. There should still be reporting read because it is beautiful, disturbing, funny or difficult to place. If attention is replaced by task completion as the universal measure, journalism's purpose has been made too small in another way.

## What this asks of newsrooms

- Tools that help reporters start with a question before producing an answer: surfacing conflicting evidence, a missing perspective, the chance to try an idea without turning it into an article.
- Describing the usefulness and limits of reporting more precisely: what has this story established, whom might it affect, what can we responsibly help someone understand, compare or do, what would change our assessment. These are editorial questions that cannot be passed to a model after publication.
- Working with designers and developers on how reporting could behave like a service, and knowing when a helpful suggestion becomes a nudge towards an outcome, and when journalism turns into activism.
- Responsibility continues after publication. A calculation goes out of date; a correction may affect an answer already received. Someone must maintain these capabilities and withdraw them when their basis no longer holds.

## A new sense of value

What people might value enough to support, beyond access to more content: being done, a product that respects their time enough to end; being able to act, with a newsroom accountable for the reporting and the tools; being surprised, by a trusted source that regularly makes their world larger; and being able to trust the silence, knowing a story is still being followed without being disturbed until something that matters has changed.
