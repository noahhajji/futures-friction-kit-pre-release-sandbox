# Worldview

How people see the world, with an emphasis on the unconscious assumptions and biases they do not call into question.

- Worldviews are shaped by the culture, experiences, norms and mindsets we carry with us, and influence our habitual ways of thinking and perceiving our situations and reality.
- Becoming aware of our own worldview, and holding space for those of others, who may make sense of the world, and of the future, quite differently from ourselves, is an important part of foresight.
- It helps people become mindful of the 'frame' they are using, including what is left out of that frame.

## The official future

- Views of the future very often tell more about the era, or zeitgeist, in which they were formulated than about the future. Attempts to foresee the future tend to be good on technological aspects while remaining stuck in their own time on behaviour, perceptions and social attitudes.
- Dominant images of the future risk becoming the 'official future', and can discredit and delegitimise other futures.
- A critical futures thinker is attentive to assumptions about the future that are rooted in the present and path-dependent, because they are sometimes incorrect or misguided: frozen in time as the world has shifted around them.

## Diversifying the future

- Diversifying the future means questioning the futures methods we use, since many traditional futures studies methodologies and frameworks are rooted in western-centric views; being comfortable challenging our own worldviews, since they set the boundaries of what we believe is possible; and ultimately widening the lens of what the future might hold.
