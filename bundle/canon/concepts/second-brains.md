# Second brains

Working with media organisations shows how difficult it is to connect innovation across teams and build on what others have learned. Ideas struggle to survive daily operations or find support beyond the team that developed them; those that challenge the current business are measured by metrics that overlook their future value; others may never learn what a team tried or which assumptions shaped its decisions. An unsuccessful experiment can still produce valuable learning if the context needed to understand it is preserved, and a good idea that does not fit one project may be gold in another.

## The weight of history in news organisations

- Protecting sources, keeping a scoop from competitors and guarding rights are important parts of the work and culture, and those instincts shape how knowledge moves inside the organisation. Competition between desks can make sharing harder, and the reasoning behind a project may stay with the people who made it.
- There is also exhaustion from the ongoing waves of change, from print to web, mobile and platforms. Any practice that asks media organisations to share and question what they know must be built within the boundaries source protection and rights require, and with as little extra effort as possible.
- Where to start when people are exhausted and used to protecting knowledge: by making it easier to learn from what others have tried and why, bringing that learning into existing workflows.

## The concept

- A second brain is a way of organising knowledge outside our heads so we can return to it, connect it and use it in new situations. The term was popularised by Tiago Forte for personal knowledge management; the ambition is older. Vannevar Bush's memex of 1945 was a desk-like device for storing books, records and communications, connected through trails of association that could be revisited and shared.
- It starts with collecting contextual data: the purpose of an experiment, the audience insights it drew on, what was tried, why a decision was made and where people disagreed. A report saying "user demand was low" tells us little about why; the context gives another team something to work with.
- Second brains operate at connected levels: individual second brains holding employees' own knowledge; team second brains capturing shared experiments and learning; and an organisational second brain preserving strategic reasoning across projects and connecting innovation to the organisation's purpose and values.
- Andrej Karpathy's LLM Wiki is a related approach: a collection of linked text files that an AI model writes, organises and updates from source material, while people select the sources and guide the work and the original material stays separate from the generated knowledge.

## Context as infrastructure

- Making context machine-readable lets people and AI agents work with it across projects. Sources, dates and the conditions behind a conclusion need to travel with it, as does the distinction between evidence and interpretation; otherwise a confident interpretation could become accepted as evidence simply through repeated retrieval.
- Formats and protocols are emerging to support parts of this, for structuring knowledge, connecting AI applications to data and tools, and communication between agents. They address technical layers; they do not settle organisational choices about access, ownership or interpretation.

## How it could work

In Hvitved's imagined media organisation of 2030: a journalist and a colleague record a conversation about a new local information service and process it on a local device, where it stays private. An agent suggests an outline for her individual second brain, capturing the idea, the reasoning and the open questions; she decides which parts to share, and the approved outline enters the team second brain while her notes and the recording stay hers. Contributing an idea does not give the organisation access to everything behind it. The team draws on the organisational second brain, and within minutes an agent finds an experiment from four years earlier whose summary says demand was low, while the original evaluation shows people found the service useful but disliked installing yet another proprietary app. The team can see what was tried, why it was judged unsuccessful and whether that conclusion is relevant now, along with the strategic reasoning behind the earlier project. Decisions, reasoning and unanswered questions enter the team second brain; learning that could help other projects reaches the organisational one, and residents can follow the reasoning the team chooses to share and contribute their own experience.

## Where to start

- Start with one current innovation project. Bring together the relevant context: earlier experiments, audience insights, signals of change and the reasoning behind the proposal. Make it machine-readable and preserve its sources.
- Agree who controls the knowledge, what can be shared and reused, which AI systems can access it and how you retain control if you change providers. Source protection, privacy, copyright and cybersecurity shape these choices from the start: what stays local and what goes through cloud services, what can be exported to other systems to avoid lock-in, who owns what.
- Begin in a controlled environment with limited access and carefully selected material, then assess what can be connected more widely.

## Key things to consider

- The efficiency AI brings to collecting, connecting and retrieving knowledge can go to cuts or to thinking, meeting colleagues and spending time with the public; in the imagined organisation, leadership has protected that time and recognised contributions to collective learning.
- Many conditions would need to hold for this to take shape, and it is unlikely to unfold exactly as imagined. The value of the scenario is to explore what could change and what to prepare for, including the choices about ownership, time and resources that make this way of working possible.
