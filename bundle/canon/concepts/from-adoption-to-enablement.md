# From adoption to enablement

Two years after an enterprise AI assistant was rolled out to every employee, with training modules and superusers demonstrating best practice, a colleague asks whether the organisation has access to it. Olle Zachrison's retrospective on six years of editorial AI leadership at Swedish Radio and BBC News starts there: AI is on everyone's lips, but how, when and where to use it at scale in editorial work is a nut the industry has not cracked.

## What the first years got wrong

- Four kinds of colleague: those too tied up in daily production to explore; the grumpily sceptical; the fierce opponents, a smaller group than some claim; and the pioneers, who built mind-boggling things from the first days after ChatGPT and rarely brought them into the light, for fear of being caught with non-sanctioned tools or accused of cheating, and because there was neither a culture nor time for sharing.
- Leaders rushed between forums and conferences, focused on theoretical possibilities and risks, and rarely talked to people further down the organisation about their needs, fears and dilemmas. Responsibility for practically exploring AI was left to the individual.
- The shiny new things: summary formats the audience had not asked for, archives you could ask anything though a search engine did it better, illustrations with six fingers. Enthusiasm and improvisation rather than strategic focus, under a super-narrative that the race was on.
- The policy trap. Much of 2023 and 2024 went into guidelines emphasising a human in the loop. Some of that was necessary, especially for public service trust, but the guidelines started from what not to do rather than what value the technology could add, and came with no direction on what to invest in. Guidelines are not strategy, and strategy must be matched with resources to implement it.
- The curse of the central innovation team: projects that could create value, but tinkering around the edges rather than rallying behind the direction, because involving hundreds of journalists takes time and blue-sky thinking was more fun.
- Meanwhile the news factory kept the lights on under an unprecedented news flow, new formats on more platforms and disappearing chairs. The middle managers were forgotten more than anyone: engagement went from the executive team straight to individuals, and nobody expected a manager to plan how AI would enter the team's core processes.

## What the organisation actually wanted

- A free-text question at the end of an introductory course, what would you use a perfect AI assistant for, drew 1,224 ideas from Swedish Radio employees, many smarter than anything a central team could devise. Research support, editorial feedback and audio processing featured; by far the largest area was administration: reporting, email, scheduling. Everyone wanted to free up time.
- A simple direction helped: use AI to improve and develop the journalism, create better user experiences, improve internal productivity.

## Launch is not use

- Switching on a feature for thousands of busy journalists does not mean they use it. Mandatory training and lunch sessions do not mean active participation; journalists are news avoiders when it comes to internal information. Critical research into how tech giants' assistants distort journalism, however valuable, makes reporters less inclined to embrace those same assistants the next day.
- Superusers, once asked, said: we do not know what we are allowed to do; AI is almost never discussed in the morning meeting; we want practical examples from other teams; the enterprise tool is hard to use for journalism. And good examples sprang up once attention turned their way, often built with home-made, unsustainable solutions by innovators navigating access, legal, security and governance barriers alone.
- Organisations that understood early that their own AI toolkit was central to broad adoption, a bespoke interface with the company's own branding, gave journalists the seal of approval they needed to feel safe. Bring AI to journalists rather than the other way around; like audiences, journalists care more about the user experience than the sophistication of the model.

## The next phase

- Three elements must go hand in hand: easy-to-use interfaces, hands-on coaching and continuous skills development.
- The possibility ahead is hyper-personalisation for audiences, moving from mass media towards an offering tailored to interests, intentions, behaviour and demographics, and for journalists, AI support adapting to each person's role, task and needs like a personal coach, with every journalist able to build and use their own tools at every step, breaking the code monopoly. A prototype multi-agent system scraping, summarising and translating social media from a dictatorship, built as a vibe-coded content system for one story, is a glimpse.
- The darker side: when everyone can build personal agents, nobody yet knows how to make sure they follow the journalistic guidelines; and whether quality journalism will reach people, be recognised as coming from its maker, or fragment society further through hyper-personalisation, is open.
- What to protect is not formats or the organisations themselves but the values: careful, methodical work with facts, a critical mindset, engaging storytelling, openness to perspectives, including underserved groups, accountability. The deskilling to worry about is not forgetting how to write but losing the ability to interact with people out in the world.

## The change in language

- Talk about enablement, giving people the chance to engage with new technologies, rather than adoption, which casts them as passive.
- Focus on local usefulness over centrally developed, ready-made solutions; let go of the fixation on formats and artefacts and talk about values and mission; make the strategic direction clearer rather than placing responsibility on the individual, which is where centralisation is actually needed; share inspiring examples from peers; connect journalists with specialist expertise elsewhere in the organisation.
- It is the people. An inspired, hard-working group who want to do something together can move mountains, and if they do not make it all the way, they will have tried.
