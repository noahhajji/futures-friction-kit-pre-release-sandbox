# The agentic information economy

There are no altars to the gods of showers, card payments or traffic lights. They are among the great achievements of modern life and almost entirely invisible, noticed only when they break. Shuwei Fang's argument is that the information environment of the near future should work the same way: within ten years AI could leave society far better informed, and almost nobody would notice. A new mother six months in, too tired to be informed, has an agent that knows the childcare rules have changed, works out what she is entitled to, drafts the application, tells her the morning a local investigation exposes failings at her maternity unit, and can show her where each claim came from and who checked it. The decisions remain hers.

## What changes

- Machines become the primary audience for information, and information changes category: from a final good read by a person who may or may not act, to an intermediate input into a decision, a transaction or an automated process, often never rendered for a human at all.
- Together these give rise to an agentic information economy, the epistemic bedrock of the wider agentic economy. It could be far bigger than today's information economy, because machines consume without the limits of human attention, and far more complex.

## The price of being right

- When a person misreads something, the error becomes an impression. When an agent acts on it, the error becomes an action: a wrong purchase, a missed entitlement, a recalled product back on the shelf. Outcomes loop back as inputs, so errors compound at machine speed and scale, and because many systems draw on the same few models, they can fail together. Correlated, automated error is a different class of risk from misinformation.
- It is also a different class of opportunity. For as long as there has been a digital information market, being right and sounding right have fetched roughly the same price, except in finance and a few high-stakes niches. Once the cost of being wrong becomes measurable, provenance, accuracy and the capacity to self-correct could become things that are priced. Being right could start to pay.
- The best version needs conditions that will not emerge by default: ownership and control spread out rather than concentrated, no moral hazard in how information is gathered and verified, and a myriad of native protocols, institutions and rules.

## Finance as the comparison

- Most people have only a folk understanding of finance, yet hand it their salaries and pensions, because protocols, institutions and regulation let them delegate trust, legibility and risk.
- The agentic information economy will need analogous systems. Finance has SWIFT and shared identifiers; information needs a shared protocol and a standard unit so a claim can be identified and traced. Finance has custodians; information needs trusted holders of dispersed, niche knowledge. Finance has rating agencies; information needs calibrated confidence in its sources. Finance has clearing houses; information needs corrections that reach every system that used the original.
- These systems also decide where value flows. Value is likely to pool at the interface, with whoever knows the most about the user. With the right rails, some share of it, and of the demand signal, could flow back along the chain to those who originate, verify and maintain information, as card interchange fees send a slice of every payment back to the issuing bank.
- Finance also shows how such institutions fail: the rating agencies that blessed mortgage-backed securities before 2008 were paid by the issuers they rated. Journalism and science encode the opposite instincts, independence from those you cover, disclosure of sources and methods, scrutiny by peers, public correction. Those are the right blueprints; whether they are used is being decided while the standards are still soft.

## Roles for journalism

- Journalism's institutions already hold much of the DNA: verification, editorial standards, archives, corrections policies and a working ethic of accountability. Within ten years they could be originating machine-ready, verified events with provenance attached; acting as custodians of a community's local and niche knowledge; rating sources and attaching calibrated confidence to claims; underwriting the information agents act on, with liability attached; and running the clearing systems that carry a correction to every system that used the original.
- A system this consequential and opaque needs watchdogs of its own. Covering the agentic information economy becomes a beat, as finance and technology are today. Someone will still need to hold information power to account; the power will simply be harder to see.
- None of this displaces storytelling. Stories are how people come to care, and they will flourish alongside. There is room for a new discipline of information and knowledge that no existing field fully addresses.

## What leaders do now

- Start from first principles: ask what an agentic information economy will actually need, looking well beyond the news industry, rather than searching for a best-in-class version of something that already exists.
- Rethink what the unit of information, and therefore of value, actually is; build the economics around that; develop the skills to work with information in ways the newsroom was never designed for.
- Coordinate, and get into the rooms where the standards for this economy are being written, while they are still soft.

## Key things to consider

- Invisibility cuts both ways. A system in the background that can quietly leave you better informed can just as quietly manipulate you, and you would never know; all the more reason to build it to serve citizens rather than steer them.
- It sits uneasily with journalism's cultural identity as a visible fourth estate wearing its trust on its masthead. The role for journalism in that future may be unrecognisable.
