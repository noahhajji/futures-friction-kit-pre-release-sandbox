# AI systems and governance

## Practices

- Some organisations deliberately stay model-agnostic and multi-model, treat chatbots as one interface among many, and run retrieval-based systems over their archives or distribute journalism into conversational AI environments as a channel. (12 mentions)
- External tool builders are moving from standalone chatbots to protocol-based integrations and constellations of several tools, including ones that stress-test plans and push back on ideas. (4 mentions)
- Some organisations invest heavily in keeping sensitive data in-house, isolating test environments for frontier tools and building internal tools where data cannot legally be shared with third-party services.
- Some organisations have built a layered AI platform, combining shared data infrastructure, an agent-building layer and specialised interfaces, with chained workflows from monitoring through drafting to distribution.
- Editorial colleagues are well placed to judge whether an AI idea is editorially sound when the data work is already involved in their day-to-day.
- Colleagues delegate routine email to agents that auto-answer, and meeting notes are taken by integrated AI agents; organisations should expect more such delegation.

## Constraints

- Staff are wary of a shared organisational tool exposing their interactions: unsophisticated questions, chat histories in projects with internal politics, or individual-level scoring of innovation performance feel like surveillance and would discourage use. Willingness to share depends on trust, and anonymising contributions risks losing an individual's distinctiveness. (8 mentions)
- Security perspectives distinguish individual experimentation at personal responsibility from organisation-wide implementation, which requires governance over which data a solution may read, who may write and read, what rights an agent receives, and separation between departments; organisations prefer personal and sensitive information hosted in-house and cannot observe individual AI use beyond network traffic. (8 mentions)
- Organisations face a dilemma between testing frontier tools and complying with European regulation: compliance status changes quickly, tools previously used have been ruled non-compliant, any AI tool may need a formal approval regime, and even meeting-recording practice is questioned. (5 mentions)
- What is built for one assistant tends not to carry over to another, and which model answers is increasingly not the user's choice: providers route between their own models, free accounts get the lesser ones, and company accounts drop to a cheaper model once a limit is reached. Making a tool behave the same everywhere is described as an uphill battle. (3 mentions)
- Staff lack the capacity to verify changes an autonomous agent makes on their behalf, and the resulting structures can become hard to track; security advisers warn that an agent's actions cannot be fully predicted. (2 mentions)
- Whether productivity tools may read staff email to organise their day is an open legal and compliance question. (2 mentions)
- Mixing very different roles' data, such as marketing and editorial, could pollute shared organisational learning and make it unusable for either group.
- Current generative models are poor at distinguishing between versions of similar content and at judging when differences matter, limiting their usefulness for surfacing the right version of institutional knowledge.

## Needs

- Organisations plan to expose internal databases through protocols or APIs so tools can be built on top, structure and centralise data before analysis, and link project, issue and performance data into a layer that tracks how initiatives perform. (11 mentions)
- Organisations want AI involvement in work to be transparent and traceable, since a lack of traceability is itself a problem in current AI-assisted workflows. (3 mentions)
- Participating organisations need clarity about what co-creation commits them to and an explicit clearing step before anything said travels further. (2 mentions)

## Beliefs

- Security review can stop an innovation project even when the issue is solvable; some practitioners argue security should not be the top priority for an innovation department's tool, while others list tight access management as a requirement. (4 mentions)
- Some organisations block AI crawlers as a strategic signal that media content should not simply be collected by external AI actors, treating control over content and data as a significant strategic theme.
- The AI-native ambition is not small applications such as summaries but a different operating logic: virtual co-workers, multi-agent systems and specialised skills that let a small team or a single journalist do far more; the strategic priority is the system and architecture around the model rather than the model itself.
- A platform philosophy in use is to build platforms that let people build their own solutions rather than having a central team build everything.
- Tools that hold context and prompt the user unasked, for instance by asking whether yesterday's discussion was followed up or feedback has come in, are described as a game changer in how people work with AI, compared with tools that wait for a question.
