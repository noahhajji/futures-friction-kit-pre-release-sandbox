# Horizons, measurement and priorities

## Practices

- Some organisations keep objectives at the operational level, treat depth of reading rather than a surface metric as the meaningful engagement signal, and categorise performance signals into editorial, funnel, conversion and cost, noting retention as a missing category. (4 mentions)
- Impact of innovation work is currently assessed by gut feeling rather than a defined metric, with formalisation deferred.

## Constraints

- Existing processes, structures and people absorb organisational attention, so the organisation must keep running today's business while creating capacity for a different future model; too much innovation starves operations and too little runs the organisation into the ground. (5 mentions)
- Practitioners fear that whatever is built gets pulled back into short-term operational framing; organisations cannot jump to long-horizon futures work without formal and cultural foundations, since leadership and colleagues need concrete results before engaging with abstract or radical discussion. (5 mentions)
- Most newsrooms work only in the first horizon. Where they explore the second, it is without stated objectives or a reason beyond having to try a few things, and the third, where the largest effect is expected, is mostly left alone; work on the long horizon has hardly been seen in the media industry. (3 mentions)
- Innovation and experimentation are structurally deprioritised in favour of operations because they do not visibly create bottom-line value from day one, unlike converting subscribers or publishing a good article. (2 mentions)
- Organisations had more slack before successive cost cuts, when media felt like a lifestyle and the path from idea to test was short; a culture where long-tenured staff can ask why things are done a certain way, and an external threat sustaining urgency, are credited for innovation culture, yet organisations question themselves more than they act.
- Timing is a central problem for long-horizon initiatives: they can be too early or too late, technologically possible but organisationally immature, or right for a future shift but impossible to anchor now.

## Needs

- Most organisations have not yet reached the point of dedicating resources to longer-term research and future preparation, though some intend to work toward it. (2 mentions)
- Open questions from practitioners about measurement: whether degree of innovation can be scored and at what level, whether the number of innovations per department should be made visible to stakeholders, what data is accessible with what margin of error, how to keep analysis future-oriented, and how to know how close a project is to its goal.

## Beliefs

- Innovation fails when radical and incremental work share the same governance and KPIs: long-term work gets measured on short-term return and is stifled. Organisations should run both in parallel as distinct disciplines, measuring incremental work by KPIs and radical work by hypotheses and learning, but most lack the structure to operate two logics at once. (8 mentions)
- The print-to-digital transition is the precedent: new activity treated as a marginal experiment while the legacy business keeps attention and power. Several organisations therefore separate radical work from the core, as a spun-out unit, a fully isolated fast track or a small 'speedboat' beside the 'oil tanker', and merge it back only once it dominates. (7 mentions)
- A three-horizon logic in use: H1 with short horizons, clear ROI and focus on the existing business; H2 connecting new initiatives to products and scaling; H3 as light, cheap experiments under high uncertainty where traditional measures fail and metrics should track learning, assumptions tested, speed to experiment and the ability to stop or promote initiatives. Visible H1 wins are used to earn permission for H2 and H3. (7 mentions)
- Innovation in media is too often optimisation disguised as innovation: the existing approach is improved but its premise never questioned; organisations are better prepared for sustaining than transformative innovation, which requires changing how journalism is produced and how humans and machines combine, not isolated experiments. (7 mentions)
- The dominant measure of success for new initiatives is near-term savings in time or money and a demonstrable business case; even projects with UX and technology ready can lack prioritisation because leadership funds what saves money now, and for top management the bottom line comes first with culture treated as secondary. (4 mentions)
- Organisations tend to bet everything on a single AI tool and stop if it fails; the alternative is many light, cheap parallel experiments with clear metrics, stopping quickly where there is no potential and reinvesting behind what succeeds. (4 mentions)
- Evolutionary, step-by-step change management remains useful but can be too slow relative to the pace of technological and market change.
- Whatever a media organisation builds with AI should launch within a couple of months rather than as a long theoretical build, because audiences are already far ahead.
- Traditional reach metrics such as page views measure a container of consumption that is shifting as human visits decline and AI-agent visits rise; a new paradigm centred on usefulness and retrieval is needed, and the right KPIs differ by type of organisation. The Nokia analogy is used: measuring a categorically new thing against old metrics.
- Practitioners accept a meaningful margin of error in data and would work with an assistant that is right roughly nine times in ten.
- At the scale of the whole organisation a single piece of work has negligible cost and value; zooming in to effort changes the picture.
