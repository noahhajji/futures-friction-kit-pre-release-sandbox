# Leadership and mandate

## Practices

- Building leadership buy-in involves joint presentations, stress-testing the pitch against a strictly numbers-focused stakeholder, and investing time to understand collaborators' differing needs in a complex multi-stakeholder structure; teams can wait a long time to get an audience with senior management. (3 mentions)
- Some organisations experience their constraint as time and resources rather than culture or structure, and see their competitive advantage as speed: a short distance from spotting a need to acting on it, fast decisions on partial information, ideas resolved informally. They fear any formal innovation tool that slows this down, and note the industry's low tolerance for friction.
- Presenting current and future AI use to leadership prompts employees to bring forward their own ideas but also overwhelms some; innovation leads count more failures than successes and see the minimum value of a shared horizons vocabulary as letting leadership acknowledge where the organisation is not yet operating.

## Constraints

- Innovation drive is concentrated in a few key individuals; ideas survive because a specific person carries them, and when that person leaves the capacity goes with them, which is a reason to formalise tacit process knowledge and move from person-dependent to collective innovation. (7 mentions)
- The largest barrier to futures-oriented innovation is human: culture, leadership, understanding and will. Long-term success depends on who holds decision power, what they are measured on, and whether a leader can justify diverting resources from operations and defend investments with no return in the next quarter. (4 mentions)
- Leadership is often absent from innovation work, so decisions get reduced to simple commercial questions; friction also arises from different leaders owning different parts of the organisation.
- Framing AI mainly as a cost-cutting lever, premised on how many editorial staff could be made redundant, makes the work impossible to introduce without provoking fear.
- Incentives at the top are structurally misaligned: a leader whose tenure will end before long-term risks materialise does not feel their urgency.
- Organisations publicly insist no one will be laid off while restructuring continues regardless, leaving staff distrustful of messaging about transformation already under way at the strategic level.

## Needs

- Innovation often happens only under clear pressure; to create legitimate urgency before a crisis is visible, leadership must be able to explain why the existing model is under pressure, which shifts could be decisive, what is risked by waiting and by investing too early, making uncertainties concrete without artificial panic. (4 mentions)
- Innovation across organisational boundaries needs an incentive structure that lets people dare to cross them, clarity about the gatekeepers, and a culture that is open without becoming too collective or uniform. (3 mentions)
- Long-horizon work must be communicable to management and the board: arguments linked to strategy and risk, a visualised portfolio, relevant metrics, small experiments presented as risk reduction, and language for uncertainty that does not sound vague; innovation leads want a tool that helps them present where the organisation is heading. (3 mentions)
- Board and top management must be committed to moving innovation between horizons and must understand, believe in and personally use any tool, translated into their own language and connected to concrete decisions; without ownership at the top, long-horizon work becomes isolated.

## Beliefs

- New solutions only create value if they are simple to implement, sit inside a familiar work situation and give the user a clear early 'wow moment'. (7 mentions)
- Established companies rationally allocate capital to their best customers and underinvest in disruptive innovation, retreating upmarket when threatened; transformation cannot show on a quarterly cycle and boards lack patience for eighteen-month cycles, and while a 70-20-10 allocation is cited, organisations land closer to 99-1-0. (6 mentions)
- The real barrier to senior decision-makers keeping pace with AI is not lack of time but lack of interest or a cautious mindset; some senior figures lack basic understanding of what the tools can do, and keeping pace ought to be treated as a decision rather than something crowded out. (3 mentions)
- Adopting a provocative, future-oriented tool depends on leaders having enough capacity to tolerate being shown that current practice may be wrong; buy-in requires a clear 'what's in it for me', an open mindset and willingness to relinquish control. (2 mentions)
- Senior leadership frames the situation as 'change or die' or 'adapt or die': the organisation cannot run its business for another five years as it has, and the decisive factor is the will to act and learn through implementation rather than having ideas.
- Innovation leaders often see their own strength as making change happen in phases rather than spotting opportunities first.
- The open question for the industry is whether it can build capabilities, culture and structure in time, which may require centralising power and taking decisions unpopular with journalists; media leadership is rarely trained in structured methods and needs more diverse backgrounds.
- An organisation's central challenge cannot be solved by AI alone, since AI has no intention of its own; the underlying issue is organisational clarity about purpose and trust rather than processing speed.
- Making innovation decision-making too collective, standardised and top-down with a near-term cost focus kills experimental ideas before they can live, unless they already have supporting data.
