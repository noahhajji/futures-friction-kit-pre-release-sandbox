# The cyber security of AI-mediated infrastructures

As AI is deployed across organisational and societal infrastructures, cyber security moves from a technical concern to a strategic one. The attack surface is expanding fast, not only because organisations are adopting more AI, but because AI is increasingly connected to the systems that shape decisions, workflows, access and operations.

## New risks

- Synthetic media can be used to impersonate executives, deceive employees, and manipulate public trust at scale.
- Prompt injection and jailbreaks can push AI systems to ignore safeguards, leak sensitive information, or take unintended actions.
- Data entered into AI tools can expose confidential knowledge, while agentic systems connected to email, files, browsers, workflows or financial tools create new paths for exploitation if they are compromised.
- Poisoned data, manipulated retrieval sources and corrupted models can distort outputs from within, quietly undermining decision making without triggering immediate alarm.

## Old and new vulnerabilities together

- Traditional risks such as phishing, credential theft and infrastructure compromise remain in place, now joined by model manipulation, synthetic identity, automated exploitation, retrieval poisoning, and system wide weaknesses caused by tightly coupled AI infrastructures.
- The challenge is intensified by the dual use nature of many of these technologies. The same software can enable productivity, surveillance, misinformation, disruption or defence, depending on who controls it and for what purpose. This mirrors the wider concern that emerging technologies blur civilian and security applications.

## Security as preparedness

- Cyber security needs to be understood as part of organisational preparedness. It is not enough to defend against known threats.
- Organisations need to ask how AI-mediated systems behave under pressure, misuse, conflict or failure, and how much opacity, dependency and loss of control they are willing to accept in exchange for speed and efficiency.
- Resilience, fallback mechanisms, verification and governance become just as important as prevention.

## Across the horizons

In Horizon 1, the priority is securing current tools, data and workflows against emerging AI enabled threats. In Horizon 2, organisations need to redesign processes and oversight models to make human and AI collaboration more resilient. In Horizon 3, they may need to prepare for an environment where cyber conflict, synthetic manipulation, information disorder and contested digital infrastructures are persistent conditions of competition and governance.

## This includes

- Cybersecurity
- AI security
- Dual use technologies

## Questions

- How does the growth of AI-mediated infrastructure change our threat landscape, not only technically but strategically?
- Which systems or workflows create unacceptable levels of dependency or opacity if they are compromised?
- How can we design for resilience, fallback, and recovery rather than assuming continuous stability?
- What governance is needed to manage dual use risks and the blurred boundaries between civilian, commercial, and security applications?
- How do we build leadership awareness of AI security issues before they escalate into reputational or operational crises?
