# Futures triangle

The Futures Triangle, developed by Sohail Inayatullah, serves as an intuitive tool to guide our thinking and help us map the dynamics at play when change occurs across the three dimensions that shape future outcomes: the push of the present, the pull from the future and the weight of history. The tension and interaction between these three forces highlights that the way future change occurs depends on the outcome of the 'friction field' created between different forces. This helps us recognise that change is often not straightforward, and always multifaceted, as it is shaped by interacting forces.

## The three dimensions

### Push of the present

- Represents what is currently and evidently influencing future change in the external environment.
- Which trends and developments are creating change and carving out the trajectories ahead?

### Pull from the future

- Represents the novel developments and images of the future that may show up in the form of new assumptions and realities.
- Which matters at the margin of current thinking do not seem to fit into existing patterns? What visions and images are pulling us towards particular futures?

### Weight of history

- Represents the constraints and legacies of the past that shape the current situation and influence future possibilities, both in negative and positive terms.
- What are the deeply rooted structures that resist change? What are the dominant narratives that hinder progress? What are the sources of stability and values that are desirable to preserve and sustain?

## Use the Futures Triangle to

- Understand dynamics of change as a reflection of the tension and interaction of different forces and visualise the complexity inherent in how change occurs.
- Inquire into how the past has shaped the current system, and how deeply rooted structures and assumptions continue to influence future possibilities.
- Facilitate a process of thinking beyond the now and exploring novel developments and signals of change that are not always obvious in the present.

## Key things to consider

- The Futures Triangle is a simple tool that can be deployed anywhere, with minimal resources required. It is particularly useful in a collaborative setting where people can bring diverse perspectives to the table and build on each other's thinking.
- Be mindful that this is an explorative tool to help guide thinking and reveal how different dynamics work together to shape how the future may unfold.
- The primary focus of the exercise should not be to categorise and place things correctly along the three dimensions. It is the process of exploring the different dimensions of change that is most important.
- After you have explored the three corners of the triangle, have a discussion about the tensions and interactions that you encounter between the three forces. This should give you a deeper understanding of the complex dynamics of change that emerge within the triangle. Consider looking for paradoxes and friction points as well as factors that enable each other. It is a healthy sign if your triangle reveals paradoxes and aspects that are counterintuitive and point in 'opposite' directions.
- One way to surface these tensions is to zoom out and look across the triangle as a whole. This helps reveal where several entries share a common root, an underlying driver, even as they carry opposite charges, pulling in different directions across the corners. Identifying shared drivers beneath these surface signals is often what reveals where the deepest tensions lie. These drivers can then be carried forward and prioritised by their impact and uncertainty, as described in more detail as part of the Uncertainties Assessment tool.
- Exploring dynamics of change by using the Futures Triangle is relatively straightforward. It relies on insight, but also on intuition and divergent thinking, especially when it comes to the pull from the future. This can feel counterintuitive to those who are more practised in evidence-based strategic thinking.

## Guiding questions for each corner

Push of the present:

- What notable trends and developments are evidently changing the future, at least somewhere in the world?
- What is pushing or driving change forward? What is happening in the contextual and industry environment?
- What is the nature and pace of change?

Pull from the future:

- What signals of change, discontinuities and novel shifts are pulling us towards particular futures?
- Which matters at the margin of current thinking do not seem to fit into existing patterns?
- Are there competing images or visions of the future?

Weight of history:

- What are the deeply rooted structures that resist change, such as prevailing cultural norms and values?
- What are the dominant narratives that hinder progress?
- What are the sources of stability and values that are desirable to preserve and sustain?

The interplay between the pull, the push and the weight creates the space of possible futures. It shows where momentum lies, where resistance exists and how clear the vision of the future is.

## Running it in a room

- Start with a silent brainstorm of around five minutes, one observation per note, considering both the contextual environment of macro forces and the operational environment of industry-related forces.
- Then discuss and collectively map the observations onto the triangle, adding new notes as perspectives emerge in the discussion.
- Remember that a lot can change, and a lot can stay the same, between now and the year in focus.

## From exploration to future issues of relevance

The triangle can be revisited to move from an explorative mapping to identifying future issues of strategic relevance. This is highly iterative and there is no fixed methodology. It is typically a discussion of the dynamics of change explored, looking for:

- Interactions of trends, visions and legacies that point in opposite directions, or that accelerate developments against the current trajectory.
- Paradoxes, logical opposites or dualities; friction.
- 'Either/or' questions about future developments or states that are surrounded by a lot of uncertainty.
- Key aspects or questions that leaders would most like to ask an 'oracle' about.
- What the people involved find interesting or of relevance to the scope or the sought-after strategic insight. This pragmatic take may feel compromising, but for a process and its scenarios to be used effectively, participants must be convinced and buy in; only then is there a chance they will contribute to decisions and actions.

## Exercise sheet: What is pushing, pulling and holding us back?

One of the kit's optional exercises: a one-page canvas with short instructions and space to work, for use with pen, paper and conversation, or alongside the assistant.

- Start with: one decision or change and any relevant evidence. Name the decision or change, first write all your observations and issues of relevance to it, then map them as trends, novel developments and weights.
- The canvas: a triangle with pull from the future at the top, push of the present at the lower left, weight of history at the lower right and plausible futures in the centre.
- At the end: a map of push, pull and weight, and one relationship that deserves attention.
