# Delphi method

A Delphi study is a collaborative foresight method designed to elicit consensus building among a panel of experts on a series of future hypotheses and propositions. The method is based on the principle that the collective foresight from a carefully selected group of experts, the Delphi panellists, provides superior insights and orientation on potential future developments compared to individual judgements and forecasts. This is especially true when dealing with complex areas with a high degree of uncertainty and incomplete knowledge.

## How it works

- While Delphi study applications are diverse, at its core the Delphi method is a controlled debate carried out over two or more iterative learning rounds.
- The first Delphi round establishes the group's initial views.
- In the second and subsequent rounds, feedback from the previous round, including current overall panel consensus measures and anonymised answers, is provided to all participants. This allows them to learn from the views of others and further develop their own opinions and reasoning based on their co-participants' input.
- A Delphi panel will usually move towards some level of consensus over several rounds. But even when this does not occur, the reasons for disparate positions and any high level of uncertainty become clear.
- The anonymity of a Delphi study is a key characteristic. Participants can know who else is involved, but not what they have said. This helps avoid groupthink and ensures that the process is not influenced by biased group dynamics.

## Real-time Delphi

- A less resource-intensive variant is the real-time Delphi method, which does not require multiple rounds.
- It leverages a digital platform to facilitate continuous, asynchronous interaction in the panel.
- This approach allows participants to monitor the panel's overall opinion and shifting consensus, and view comments and arguments made by other participants in real time.

## Use the Delphi method to

- Leverage the collective intelligence from a diverse group of experts.
- Provide orientation on complex future issues by understanding where expert judgement converges, where it diverges, and where genuine uncertainty remains.
- Support informed decision-making by providing structured expert judgement and a deeper understanding of key future issues.

## Key things to consider

- Delphi studies are difficult to do well, and a poorly designed Delphi can provoke antagonism and leave you with poor-quality insights. Therefore, a great deal of attention must be given to the choice of panellists. The experts involved need to be selected based on their knowledge and experience so that they can contribute with valuable insights, while also representing diversity of perspectives. It is crucial to bring in different areas of expertise related to the same focal issue to enrich the discussion and enhance the quality of the findings.
- The future hypotheses/propositions and their answer scales included in the Delphi must be meticulously prepared and tested to avoid ambiguity. The quality of the results depends on all panellists interpreting each hypothesis in the same way. If a hypothesis is ambiguous, panellists will respond based on their own differing interpretations, making the results difficult to trust.
- Delphi studies always tackle issues formulated in hypotheses/propositions about which uncertain and incomplete knowledge exists, and unlike traditional questionnaires, they do not rely on direct questions. The panellists should be prompted with the importance of providing elaborate argumentation for their choices and assessments, for the other panellists to consider in subsequent rounds.
- Normally, the number of participants in a Delphi is relatively small. Consequently, a Delphi study does not (and is not intended to) produce statistically significant results across a representative sample. It represents the synthesis of opinion of the group of panellists, no more, no less.
- The Delphi method is relatively complex to execute. It is a time-consuming and resource-intensive process and may therefore not be appropriate for projects with a short timeline or limited resources.

At the top, Delphi preparation spans both tracks: recruiting the Delphi panel based on identified selection criteria, and developing futures hypotheses/propositions. The round-based track runs through four boxes in sequence: distribution round 1 (collecting responses from the initial survey), response analysis round 1 (analysing responses from round 1 and preparing feedback for round 2), distribution round 2 (collecting responses from the refined survey with open results from round 1), and response analysis round 2 (analysing responses to potentially feed into further rounds).
