# Uncertainties assessment

A fundamental aspect of strategic foresight is identifying and engaging with critical uncertainties for the purpose of longer-term strategic planning. Critical uncertainties are high-impact drivers with uncertain outcomes that can significantly influence the future. To identify these uncertainties, the Impact/Uncertainty matrix can be used to assess drivers based on their impact and degree of uncertainty in relation to the focal issue. Identifying critical uncertainties often serves as the starting point for defining axes of uncertainty, which are then used in scenario building to explore different possible futures.

## How it works

- Uncertainties are different from trends in that they involve a lack of predictability regarding the future direction, strength and/or outcomes of a particular driver. Uncertainty, in this context, refers to the unpredictability of how things might manifest in the future.
- Hence, we should be able to identify two 'extreme' but plausible directions of development or outcomes, polarities, by describing alternative ways that a driver might bring change.
- Uncertainties are then deemed critical when they are also assessed to have high strategic impact.

## The quadrants of the Impact/Uncertainty matrix

- High-impact/low-uncertainty drivers (top left quadrant): the high-impact drivers with relative certainty. These are factors that should be monitored closely and preferably acted on in current strategic planning.
- High-impact/high-uncertainty drivers (top right quadrant): the critical uncertainties. They could shape very different futures, so longer-term planning should seek to anticipate and prepare for them.
- Low-impact drivers (bottom quadrants): secondary drivers that, for the time being, can be disregarded for the purpose of strategic foresight. They should still be monitored, as they may develop into higher-impact drivers over time.

## Use uncertainties assessment to

- Evaluate which drivers are surrounded by more or less certainty and identify strategically important critical uncertainties.
- Help question the assumptions we have about the direction of the future and guide decision-making under uncertainty.
- Lay the groundwork for scenario development, where two critical uncertainties form the axes of a scenario matrix.

## Key things to consider

- A driver is assessed to have high impact (on the 'impact' axis) when it possesses the ability to fundamentally alter the business environment or the focal issue. This axis is often used in relative terms to help prioritise the drivers with the most significant potential to drive fundamental change.
- Be especially aware of how the 'degree of uncertainty' is defined in this context. A driver is assessed to have a low degree of uncertainty when you find reasonable certainty around its future direction of development. On the other hand, a driver is assessed to have a high degree of uncertainty when you are very uncertain about the future direction in which it will bring change. In other words, a driver is deemed uncertain when we are faced with alternative ways it might bring change in the future.
- Be aware of how our biases affect the assessment of uncertainties. You should always refrain from claiming that something is not uncertain (low degree of uncertainty) just because one of the outcomes is less desirable for you or your organisation.
- It is not easy to identify genuine critical uncertainties. Hence, for the critical uncertainties (top right quadrant) you always need to have a discussion to crystallise what exactly it is you are uncertain about in relation to the specific driver. There is likely more than one uncertain factor related to a critical uncertainty. This depends on the level of granularity at which an uncertainty is described. Also, check for the possibility to group any related critical uncertainties, to make sure that you don't have different versions of the same uncertainty.
- The Impact/Uncertainty matrix can be used in two different ways. Sometimes you have a consolidated list of drivers that you want to map according to their impact and uncertainty to identify the critical uncertainties before you describe alternative ways that a driver might bring change (i.e. define polarities). At other times you may want to use the matrix as a prioritisation mechanism of already defined uncertainties with polarities.

## From issues to critical uncertainties

Assessing critical uncertainties is a deductive phase that usually follows the exploration of trends and dynamics of change.

- A future issue is broader in scope than a trend. It encapsulates interactions, frictions and sometimes paradoxes between related underlying driving forces, where future developments are ambiguous or unclear, yet where the potential developments carry relevance for the future in focus.
- For each issue, the discussion crystallises what it is we are uncertain about, and whether two 'extreme' but plausible sets of outcomes, polarities, can be meaningfully identified.
- There can be more than one uncertain factor related to each issue, depending on the level of granularity at which an uncertainty is described. Check for the possibility to group related uncertainties, so that there are not different versions of the same uncertainty.
- Issues with high impact and high uncertainty are the critical uncertainties, and identifying them is often the outset for defining the axes of uncertainty for scenario building.

| Future issue | Uncertainty | Polarities |
|---|---|---|
| Access versus ownership | How will end-users engage with asset ownership? | Access more important than ownership, or ownership still widespread |
| Urban mobility alternatives | To what extent will cars remain central in future urban mobility systems? | Cars are marginalised, or cars maintain a dominant position |

## Types of uncertainty

Uncertainties relating to an identified issue come in different types. The lines between them are not fixed, and most strategic uncertainties are a little of each, but it helps to be aware of them.

- Conceptual uncertainties: two conceptually different future states, such as global versus regional, or access versus ownership.
- Unidirectional uncertainties: where we will be at a given point, from 'now' to an 'end state'; the uncertainty lies in the degree.
- Intensity or magnitude uncertainties: 'high/low' or 'fast/slow'; also unidirectional in nature.
- Tipping point uncertainties: events or paradigm shifts.

## Definitions of impact and uncertainty

- High impact: the issue will have a significant impact and fundamentally alter the business environment or the topic in scope. Low impact: the issue will not significantly alter it.
- Low uncertainty: you are reasonably certain about the future direction of the issue, with relative certainty of one of the two directions. High uncertainty: you are very uncertain about the future direction, closer to 50/50.
- Do not claim something is not uncertain just because you do not want it to happen.
- Where you assess relatively low uncertainty, mark the polarity you think most likely.

## What each quadrant calls for

- Critical uncertainties, high impact and high uncertainty: the focus of scenario analysis; discover the uncertainty related to the issue.
- High impact issues, high impact and low uncertainty: should be considered in all scenarios and require an immediate strategic response.
- Secondary issues, low impact: should rarely be in the analysis; park them for now but monitor their development.

## Polarities as bookends

The power of working with polarities is that they provide clear contrasts. Polarities should not be seen as endpoints but as 'bookends' that help explore the plausible space in between. Be careful with false or unclear dichotomies.
