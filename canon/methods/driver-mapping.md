# Driver mapping

Driver mapping helps to identify the most influential drivers of change in a system or in relation to a specific domain or focal topic. A driver of change is an underlying force that generates or shapes a trend or development. Drivers include things like shifts in attitudes and behaviour, as well as economic and technological forces that influence how something spreads and grows.

## How it works

- Driver mapping is essentially about making sense of gathered insights, such as from a scanning exercise, and identifying leading drivers based on their nature or typology. There are different ways to do this.
- One of the most common techniques is the PESTEL framework (or other acronym variants), where drivers are mapped across six different macro dimensions.
- Alternatively, the Verge framework, developed by Richard Lum and Michele Bowman, takes an ethnographic view, mapping change by its points of impact on people.

## Use driver mapping to

- Organise and understand key drivers of change and provide a more detailed picture of future developments facing an organisation.
- Produce a set of critical or important drivers as key input into further futures work and for the creation of futures scenarios.
- Look for affinity between signals to reveal the larger drivers beneath them. Where several signals (e.g. from a scanning exercise) move in the same direction, they often point to a single underlying driver.

## Key things to consider

- Driver mapping does not have to follow a predefined framework like PESTEL or Verge. Sometimes it may be desirable to cluster drivers by your own alternative themes that emerge from your brainstorm or research that are more suitable for your specific purpose.
- A driver should be 'neutral' and allow for different outcomes. In that sense, a driver should not be framed in a positive or negative form. For example, 'job loss from automation' is not neutral, but 'impact of automation on jobs' is a suitable driver.
- Look for possibilities to eliminate duplicates and merge drivers that are too similar. Too many drivers risk a longer analysis and treating less influential drivers as influential. Conversely, too few may limit the important dimensions of the analysis and lead to a simplistic one that omits critical drivers.
- At this stage, it can sometimes be desirable to identify the most important drivers by mapping them according to their impact on the domain or topic of interest and the uncertainty of the future direction or outcome of the driver. This approach is described in more detail as part of the Uncertainties Assessment tool.

## Figure: PESTEL framework

| Dimension | Description |
|---|---|
| Political | Political factors, such as interventions in societies and economies, (geo)political tensions, development goals, etc. |
| Economic | Economic factors, such as overall economic growth, markets, inflation, etc. |
| Social | Social factors, such as demographic change, changes in people's behaviour, values and lifestyles, etc. |
| Technological | Technological advancements and their influence on society, businesses, new innovations, etc. |
| Environmental | Environmental/ecological aspects, such as the effects of climate change, loss of biodiversity, new energy sources, etc. |
| Legal | Legal/regulatory factors, such as data protection, anti-discrimination laws, global regulatory issues, etc. |

## Figure: Verge framework

| Domain | Description |
|---|---|
| Define | Concepts, ideas and paradigms we use to define ourselves and the world around us (worldviews, social values, attitudes, etc.) |
| Relate | Structures, relationships and ways of interaction which organise people and organisations (social structures, governance structures, business models, etc.) |
| Connect | Technologies and practices used to connect people, places and things (digital technologies, media, music, language, etc.) |
| Create | Processes and technologies through which we innovate, design and produce (manufacturing, engineering, life sciences, rule-making, etc.) |
| Consume | Ways in which we acquire and consume the goods and services we create (modes of exchange, consumer preferences, marketing, etc.) |
| Destroy | Ways in which we destroy value and the reasons for doing so (waste, attempts to undermine rules and norms, repression, harmful reproduction of old systems, etc.) |

## PESTEL as a checklist

The PESTEL framework can be restrictive and prescriptive in the exploratory scanning phase. Use it as a checklist instead: once the trends are gathered, check whether all six dimensions are covered and whether anything from a dimension is missing.
