# Use the liquid wisely or choose human first

## The potential and the danger in the liquid

A central part of the AI content revolution concerns liquid content. It can be defined as content that is not fixed, like an article, a book or a podcast, but always adapts to a specific need of the individual person.

An AI chat is one form, where the content is always shaped to exactly your question, but it can take many other forms: audio, infographics, video, websites, apps, even a whole internet, if that is still the right word, that can be created and adapted from the user's concrete wishes, and where the same content can flow multimodally between the forms. It is a decisively different way of telling stories and conveying information from the one journalism has been used to, a kind of return to the oral ideal from before the Gutenberg revolution took hold, and it is changing the whole internet and the way knowledge and information are created and consumed. Browsing gives way to asking, content turns fluid, creation scales while stability erodes, and attention fragments even as platforms consolidate. It challenges the linear in creative forms in favour of the user helping to create stories, or even worlds of information, adapted in real time: everything is generating, the web generates itself.

The liquid opens both a potential and a threat to journalism.

### Early experience

- Media-developed AI chats, where the user can talk with the medium's journalism, often limited to the medium's own content as quality assurance, show a potential for increased relevance. Users get a new way to search the archive and receive answers to exactly their question, adapted to their level of knowledge and need, with some potential in education. But the chats have met technical limits: answering only the questions they actually have an answer to, and not remembering the earlier dialogue. The form challenges ideals of letting people write every story and constitutes a loss of control that raises concern for publishing quality, which has led to hidden placement and unremarkable traffic. Even with prominent placement on the front page, traffic has been limited.
- Where users have asked millions of questions and traffic exceeded expectation, relatively few users asked their own open questions; they preferred buttons with pre-formulated questions. Perhaps people do not want to talk with journalism at all but rather to be served the news. Users have hardly complained about the new form, which could suggest a greater reluctance inside journalistic environments than outside among users.
- Chats have shown relatively large potential within narrower areas, such as concrete elections or big breaking stories, where usage rose considerably.
- One experiment led to a "what has happened since you last read" function, where users get the latest developments personalised since they last followed along. It has been a great success because it solves a need journalism's forms have struggled with: people arrive with different histories when they read the latest news. The success could not be calculated in advance but revealed itself only in the experiment, so the chat was turned down and the new function turned up.
- A pure AI podcast adapted to your interests and made only for you gained relatively many users and a usable quality, but closed in 2026: the commercial side proved more challenging than the technical, indicating that the business model behind conversational content is not yet easy to carry.
- Another approach treats the medium's research and data as a kind of atoms that AI can assemble in very different ways, making possible formats that put together and open up the whole research and archive in radically new ways. Here the quality of the underlying data, the medium's archive, becomes more important than the single news item, and a competitive parameter against not only other news media but also other liquid formats. Because of technical limitations the article as a form has always been a closed container, suited to people arriving at the same time with the same degree of knowledge; in reality people have very different knowledge, needs and history, which can now be met as the technical limitations disappear.

The potential for relevance, intimacy and understanding of intention is enormous, and it is risky to leave that field to the tech giants. Here is the potential for an extremely democratic form of communication, if it is structured right. People already use AI to understand complex news, make summaries, compare sources and answer follow-up questions, all user needs with journalistic potential.

### Questions the liquid raises

- In journalism the single story unit, such as the article, has always been the paper of record, where the first draft of history lies in fixed form: the archive. What is the system of record in the era of liquid content? What is the underlying data?
- How does one avoid echo chambers, or colonisation of our inner life?
- If the information we receive has already been processed by an agent, how should it choose, and by what parameters? How should the publisher influence how their work is interpreted, if the publisher has any control at all over the interface it is interpreted through?
- Understanding the user's context and intention may become the new demand signal, rather than the user's clicks or time spent as before. The more knowledge of context a news medium has, the better service it can give and the harder it becomes for the user to switch. Power lies to a high degree with whoever has the interface, the place where the user interacts with the information, rather than with the medium that delivers some of the information. Right now that interface for liquid content lies very largely with the tech platforms, which gain extremely great control and direct contact with users, leaving journalistic media trailing on relevance, intimacy and intention, even though AI is partly dependent on the media's content.
- It becomes decisive that the producers of content also gain access to the demand signal, and society needs a kind of common ground. The shared front page showed what other people were also following and so made the shared public conversation more shared. Here could lie an essential task for journalistic media, possibly in larger communities. Right now there are no shared frameworks and structures that reward quality in liquid content. A new market is forming and will be shaped by those who build now, and the disciplines that journalism and the scientific method developed over centuries, truth-seeking, accountability and self-correction, are the operating principles that market will require.

Here lies a great potential, and an equally great threat, which will require experiments, adaptation and large resources if media are to learn to speak the liquid language fluently.

## The other way: human first

There is also an alternative path to successful existential innovation: very personal content with a clear voice. Call it human-first content. Such formats have already triumphed on social media among creators, influencers and many others, and already mark news media, in some forms of expression more than others. In an AI era this type of content has even greater potential, because the thirst for real people and real voices who know what they are talking about, or at least do it authentically, only grows.

While half of the largest news sites in the United States lost a fifth of their traffic in the year to April 2026, newsletter platforms grew, perhaps because they do not sell artefacts, such as articles, but clear, trustworthy voices. News creators and influencers have seen marked growth in recent years. A key to their success is understanding the forms and formats of communication on social media: taking formats that already work there and translating them to news and politics. Young audiences have chosen video platforms, not the established media, so one has to come to them and use forms that reach them. Authenticity is a new currency, and trust arises not from striven-for objectivity but from competence, integrity and goodwill, meaning close contact with one's community.

Established media too have great potential to step far more offensively into human-first content: building trusted voices, and turning journalists into voices and stars. It is not easy, because journalistic norms and organisational history can be in tension with more personal, human forms of storytelling. Some media choose instead to make the medium the star rather than the individual journalists, even while using a fairly personal form in audio.

Some media let creators and influencers make content with them, systematically selecting already-established young creators and then training them, for example in ethics and research tools. It is far easier to go that way, beginning with creators who already have reach and who then learn journalistic standards, than to start with journalists who must learn to tell like creators. Creators are audience infrastructure: they bring new users to the medium's other offerings. When trust has no reach, it has failed, and if established media do not engage, the public service of the future will be built outside them. Such a programme is built from small, low-friction experiments that are continuously evaluated.

Classic media are taking smaller steps in the same direction: dropping the idea of writing without a byline, or building the medium around people with very clear expertise and voice. The transformation is not without difficult trade-offs. One dilemma is how to unite the pace of a daily digital publishing rhythm with clear personalities of high expertise and with fundamental culture and workflows. Another is how clear voices are united with a shared brand: is one a loose working community of original voices or a more unifying club, and in that case, a club for whom? Many media approach the same dilemma from the opposite direction, because they must learn to unite a clear media brand with the development of freer, personal voices.

At a deeper level there are parallels to the dominant storytelling forms from before the Gutenberg parenthesis. Before books, the troubadour, the always personal narrator, was at the centre, as in today's human-first content. When everything flows, we seek the clear voice. This places demands on those narrators' standards for facts and ethics, and precisely there lies a potential for news media and narrators with journalistic ballast to make something that fulfils a fundamental need in the information landscape.

## The danger in the middle position

Both paths, the liquid and the human, hold important potential. But media and journalists must choose. Bet on one or the other, or at least be very conscious of which path you choose if you work with both. The barbell strategy describes how everything that falls between the two outer poles can be squeezed so that the middle collapses. Within investment the barbell strategy is a bet on both the very safe, for most of the investment, and the very risky, for a smaller part, and here too the middle position is to be avoided.

The danger lies in half-hearted attempts at the liquid, or content that is a bit personal but at the same time fairly classical, so that one, as journalist or medium, is not really anything or anyone. Better to speed up and throw oneself into liquid experiments, or slow the pace and let human stories and voices unfold. In many other forms of communication the barbell tendency is coming through in these years: music, games, film, video and television often succeed at two extremes, very superficial and fast, or very deep and long.

Existential innovation is about choosing and taking responsibility upon oneself. That holds here too, where the middle position seems easy but carries a marked risk of irrelevance.

Used wisely and consciously, the two positions are not each other's opposites. There can be great potential in using AI tools to support human-first content. Where AI tools are widely used, the plan is that they free time to make what is judged the core of the journalism: people making so-called super content, the stories AI cannot make, which often require close human contact and often convert new subscribers. Many of the competences prized in the classic digital age, speed, fast writing, the journalist as runner, are no longer so much in demand because AI makes the first draft of much content; instead the ability to make super content is sought. So AI takes over many small tasks so that the big, important things, such as telling as a human being, can be left to humans.

The greatest potential probably lies in using AI far more directly to support human-first content, where a voice has a host of artificial agents, producers and other world-builders to lift it. The voices that manage this can gain an extreme competitive advantage in future. But it requires big experiments and courage.
