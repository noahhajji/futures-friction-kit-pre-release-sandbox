# Four responses to public scepticism of AI

The value of news is in large part in the eye of the beholder: a relationship between what journalists and publishers do and the members of the public who engage with it. That relationship is challenged from outside, by AI, by dominant platforms and in many countries by political attacks, and from inside, by an industry that tends to take it for granted. Rasmus Kleis Nielsen's typology sorts the responses available to a newsroom in a context where the public is sceptical of generative AI in news.

## The two axes

- Whether the journalist or publisher is a less or more intense user of generative AI.
- Whether they leave their choice implicit or explicitly tell the public a story about what they are doing and why.

## The four responses

- Resigned journalism: limited use of generative AI, and no explicit communication about it. Some end up here by default because they are not changing their practices; others hope for a fast-follower option. The position is complicated by the public's tendency to assume that news media already use AI intensely.
- Furtive AI journalism: increasingly intense use, and no explicit communication. Some feel the work should speak for itself and will not spend scarce resources explaining it; others want the "cheaper to make" part and privately suspect the public is right about "less transparent" and "less trustworthy" too. In the absence of explicit communication, much of the public will assume a publisher is here.
- Romantic journalism: limited use, at least on the editorial side, explicitly communicated. The romantic journalism manifesto is the clear example. It is the mirror twin of furtive AI journalism: if the public thinks AI makes news cheaper and less trustworthy, and you fear they may be right, differentiate clearly from the rest of the industry.
- Modernist journalism: increasingly intense use, explicitly communicated, in the sense "modernist" has in architecture, art and literature: make it new, in the belief that our best selves may be ahead of us. It is hard to find among established publishers, though individual journalists and projects are effectively pursuing it.

## The decision

- The proposal is not that a newsroom should commit to one response, but that it decides which comes closest to what it wants to do, and considers whether and how it communicates with the public about what it does, how, and why.
- Each response could be named differently, stoic, stealthy, retro, techno-solutionist, and each comes with risks and possible rewards. Some paths require little change; those committed to more AI use or more explicit communication require more, and probably a culture shift, since research suggests journalists often have a partial picture of how the public sees their work and do not prioritise investing in that relationship.
- Three starting questions: What does the part of the public we serve assume we do with AI? What do we do to explain what we do with AI? Would people see the principles we preach and what we practise as aligned, if they knew precisely what we do with AI?

## Key things to consider

- Communicating choices about AI is one small component of a wider, more foundational task: being clear about what you do, who you do it for and why, and keeping what you do and what you say you do aligned, explainable and intelligible to the public you want to engage.
- That wider work is cultural rather than technological. It could take longer than any AI rewiring. It could also begin tomorrow.
