# True truth as gold

When information flows and becomes personal, the need for real facts and the best attainable truth rises. That holds just as much when the world develops fast and unpredictably. If war or crisis comes, people want correct journalism about what is happening to their money or where the drones are flying. So the situation is that wrong information has probably never been greater or more used, and can explode with AI, while quality information becomes more and more valuable.

How do news media benefit from that, and how do they ensure that people get that type of information, and want to get it, even to the point of willingness to pay?

## Self-correcting mechanisms

At the largest information revolution so far, the printing press, the new mass-produced books did not at first simply create more knowledge; to a high degree they created more misinformation. Some of history's first bestsellers were books about witches that went viral and set the witch hunts in motion. Only when self-correcting mechanisms were established and gained ground did the new information technology, the book and later the newspaper, become to a higher degree a carrier of knowledge and form a decisive foundation for the development of the modern enlightened society. Self-correcting mechanisms were developed within science, journalism, the courts and other institutions, where through self-correction one systematically tries to find the best attainable version of the truth. In recent centuries these mechanisms have been decisive for supporting the quality of knowledge and information, and thus for maintaining society's conversation with itself and a reasonably healthy democracy.

Now we face a new information revolution, with an explosion in AI-created content in a wholly new, liquid form, gathered at a few tech giants, while the number of journalists has fallen. Our institutions are not necessarily built to handle that development. It creates an asymmetry: the need for truth rises, but who builds what can monetise truth and at the same time benefit society? The tech giants are at work, but what can news media do? For a hundred years journalism built an infrastructure for truth, but no one has worked out how to do it now, at high speed. And can we risk leaving the infrastructure for truth and knowledge to American AI companies?

All of this argues that journalists and news media should build the new self-correcting mechanisms of the era. It is an enormous task, and several specific challenges are decisive to handle.

### Noise as a new censorship

AI slop already floods the internet, with outright slop shops in growth. The problem concerns both quality and quantity.

- Quality: this is ultra-processed content, dopamine-dense, with at best negligible informational value and at worst corrosively negative. Where fast news was once criticised as fast food, with easy calories that quickly disappeared, this is a wholly different scale: ultra-processed, AI-boosted snacks that can be even harder to say no to and even lower in quality.
- Quantity: more information does not give more wisdom; on the contrary it risks drowning true information in cheap, false information. Here noise functions as a new kind of censorship. Human-made content can also grow markedly in volume with the assistance of AI tools. We are moving from scarcity to abundance: production of content is no longer a moat.

This opens potential for the media, both on quality and on helping to navigate the flood of content. Curation, for example, can become even more valuable. Could news media earn money on a new type of high-quality aggregation? AI can also be part of the solution, for example by creating a more diverse news diet if used right.

### What if people are indifferent?

Several studies have suggested that some news users are perfectly satisfied with the AI products even though they make frequent errors, as long as they are convenient to use. That possibly constitutes an existential challenge, because the whole structure of news media is set up to deliver precise information. Will people pay at all for the information being of slightly higher quality? Are free and convenience actually more important?

If it is to succeed, it probably requires pronounced innovation of forms and formats that give users the desire for more truthful information, and show very clearly why it is important. Here lies a great task for news media, which again concerns clarity about who they are, but also for politicians and others who support a viable press. Many very different disciplines and forces are needed if self-correcting mechanisms are to be developed that people will actually use or pay for: not just tech experts, politicians, financiers and communicators, but everyone from philosophers and anthropologists to artists and psychologists.

This does not mean that news media should throw away everything that works now. The need for change can at times be exaggerated: some of the successful formats of the moment resemble echoes of old television or radio programmes. In the hunt for shiny new AI tools one can also risk forgetting one's soul.

### Attention splinters

The shared front page is already more myth than reality for many, but it will disappear even more in the liquid and personal era. The media reality is already being atomised, and such a fragmented information landscape makes the shared public conversation harder. Where engagement has been a carrying metric for social media, intimacy may become it for AI.

That can lead to information oracles that understand exactly you extremely well, but which you do not really understand: where does the information actually come from? It is hard to fact-check or trust something we do not understand. At the same time such oracles risk isolating each person, removing the friction that is necessary to understand others' viewpoints, since it is precisely through shared language and meetings that meaning and ethics arise. It is the ultimate possible tool of manipulation. Social media that create unreal narratives help destabilise democracy itself, creating mistrust and eroding the perception of a shared reality, and generative AI fits perfectly into that dynamic.

These challenges also constitute a potential for news media. Can they create the shared front pages of the future, or communities where people gather around stories and news? Bundling of content has already proved relatively successful in several places, including shared subscriptions across media. Could that develop into a home for shared stories? One can also imagine journalistically oriented organisations developing a healthier type of information oracle, giving relevance and intimacy but set up to challenge and bring opposing viewpoints. Newer studies show how people who use AI heavily risk losing learning, because learning requires a certain friction. Can media build products that do not just give easy answers and weaken human development, but do the opposite: built to make us better in the broadest sense of the word?

## Usefulness or public good?

Much suggests that clear usefulness is essential when it comes to monetising truth in these years. Profit margins are markedly larger on selling intelligence and solutions that companies and people can use very directly than on classic journalism.

Put simply, there is money in the solution economy. But what about ordinary journalism, which one cannot necessarily use very directly in one's life? Should that, perhaps, rather be understood as a public good? The development poses new challenges to financing, and perhaps to the need for support, and possibly calls for news media to focus parts of their offering into a solution economy. Otherwise it will, as now, largely not be media but domain experts and others who build that type of information business.

Truth can be gold, also financially, for media-like companies. They can draw on strong norms and processes for producing the best attainable version of the truth. But in the changed digital infrastructure that type of content risks drowning or being hard to assess for quality, some users are becoming indifferent, and everything may become so personalised that we lose a reasonably shared perception of the state of things. That requires existential innovation. But it also holds important opportunities for journalism, if it can develop solutions to those truth problems, also for very specific target groups.

Developing the self-correcting mechanisms of the new era is an existentially important task, not only for journalists but for all of us, which is why it requires deeper community and broader collaboration.
