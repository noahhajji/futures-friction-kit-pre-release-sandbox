# Service, not publication

Journalism has been made easier to publish, faster to distribute and simpler to consume, yet it still asks an extraordinary amount of the people it serves: notice the story, recognise why it matters, remember what happened last time, find the update, work out what has actually changed. The newsroom does the difficult work of reporting and leaves much of the work of staying informed to the reader. Nikita Roy's point is that this is not an engagement problem but a limitation in how journalism has been designed. A finished article is not always a finished public service.

## Form and function

- Verification, scrutiny, explanation and helping people navigate uncertainty are journalism's functions. Articles, broadcasts, newsletters and podcasts are forms used to deliver them.
- The forms matter. A narrative investigation can reveal a pattern no collection of personalised answers could capture; a shared story can create public attention and political pressure. The point is not to replace the article but to stop treating it as the boundary of responsibility.

## A newsroom organised around questions

- Imagine a newsroom organised not only around stories to publish but around public questions it commits to follow: will the promised housing be built, what is happening to local water quality, how is a new policy affecting families. Each question has a history, sources, unresolved claims, competing accounts and developments worth watching.
- Reporting still creates the knowledge, but it becomes a living, accountable record that can be revisited, interrogated and updated as the world changes.
- The invitation changes from "read this" to "we will keep watching this". That is a more demanding promise than an alert when a new article appears: it means noticing consequential change when no article was planned, sometimes saying that nothing has been resolved, and acknowledging when the newsroom can no longer reliably keep watch.

## Making continuity sustainable

- None of this began with AI. Community-centred newsrooms have long answered individual questions and followed public promises; one has run a text-message service since 2016 that delivers housing and utilities information on demand and lets residents reach a reporter, now using AI for property lookups and to surface what residents ask about, while journalists write every message.
- What changes with AI is the range of connecting work that becomes feasible: sifting sprawling agendas, comparing revisions to public documents, connecting a development to years of reporting, translating, adapting verified explanations for different levels of familiarity.
- AI may lower the cost of connecting information, but it does not remove the cost of being responsible for it. The better the service, the more the responsibility matters: if an error travels into a text message, a translation or an audio briefing, the correction cannot live only on the original article. Editorial responsibility becomes product design.

## Bigger than a chatbot

- A service built only around answering users' questions leaves a gap: people may not know what they need to ask. Journalism matters precisely because people often do not know what is happening or which question to ask. An interface that answers perfectly formulated questions can leave the biggest blind spots untouched.
- The systems worth building combine responsiveness with editorial initiative. Personal relevance should be a doorway into public understanding, not an escape from it; people may receive different formats, languages or levels of explanation, but not different underlying facts, and no algorithm should decide that an inconvenient truth is irrelevant because nobody asked.
- The service need not look like AI at all. It might arrive as a text message, a short audio explanation or a timed update; its form is irrelevant as long as it makes journalism more useful.

## What becomes of the newsroom

- If more people encounter reporting through assistants and platforms they do not associate with the publisher, newsrooms risk becoming invisible suppliers of raw material for somebody else's product. Publishing more articles will not solve that. A newsroom's enduring value may lie in the knowledge it can stand behind: what it has verified, what remains disputed, what has changed, which questions it continues to investigate.
- The reporter remains indispensable as the person who understands context, challenges official accounts, notices what is missing and earns trust. Editors become stewards of commitments that extend across time. Product and engineering teams turn judgement into systems that can be tested, corrected and sustained. The newsroom becomes an institution that remembers, checks and returns.
- Success changes with it: not how many stories were produced or how much attention they drew, but whether people were better able to understand the forces shaping their lives, recognise what had changed and exercise their own judgement.
