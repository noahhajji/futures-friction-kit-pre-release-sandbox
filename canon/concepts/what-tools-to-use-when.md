# What tools to use when?

Given the extensive number of tools available, how does one pick the right ones? The same tool can serve different purposes, and different tools can help achieve similar ends, so knowing how to choose and combine them is much a matter of practice and situational awareness. There is no single right or wrong answer, but here are some criteria that may help with selection.

## Objective

- Be absolutely clear and intentional about what you want your futures work to deliver. Every futures project is different, and should be based on its own specific context, premises and objectives.
- Some tools are useful for opening minds to novelty and different perspectives, while others are more about creating a shared sense of the future or understanding what drives change in the present.
- Some provide insights relatively quickly and with little effort, while others are more demanding and require substantial effort.
- Some work very well in explorative workshop settings, while others are more geared towards developing or testing strategy in a decision-making context.

## Mix and match

- Combining and adapting foresight tools and methods is often necessary as part of a foresight process, as no single tool does everything.
- Different questions call for different tools, and some of these tools work better in unison than others.
- Some tools may yield an output that naturally flows as input into the next phase of the process.

## Intended users

- Know the recipients of your work as well as the people involved in the process.
- Some are more open to divergent futures thinking, while others can be more constrained in their thinking and views at the initial stage.
- Knowing both your 'intended users' and the people involved in the process or project allows you to, for example, start with 'lighter' tools that are easier to grasp, and then progress onto more challenging ones.

## Striking the balance

- This is connected to the point of knowing your intended users. At a high level, part of your role as a foresight practitioner is finding the edge of your users' comfort zone and helping push them just past that. This implies that what is considered provocative for one group may not be for another, and vice versa.
- Experience suggests that when people engage with foresight work they consider too extreme or implausible, they tend to become even more confident in their original view of the future. This can contribute to their distrust in foresight, as they might think it is too speculative to be useful.
- Conversely, if the foresight work is not challenging enough, it is easily dismissed as pointless, which again can contribute to distrust in foresight.
- For foresight work to be used efficiently, your users must be convinced of the soundness, relevance and value of the process, whatever its objective. Only then is there a chance it will influence decisions and actions.

## A fool with a tool

- A fool with a tool is still a fool. Doing foresight well sits in the sweet spot where tools, frameworks, evidence-based insights, data, knowledge and trajectories overlap with futures thinking, divergent thinking, creativity, imagination and speculation. Explorative foresight that challenges the business-as-usual future and anticipates novelty needs both.
- Foresight tools and techniques are used to guide and advance collective thinking about how the future might show up in the form of new assumptions, behaviours and realities, not to arrive at answers or outcomes that can be underlined as 'correct'. It is not about getting it right, but about doing it right.
