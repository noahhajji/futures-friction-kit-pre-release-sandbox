# Cognitive biases in futures thinking

Biases are subconscious errors in our thinking that lead us to misinterpret information from the world around us. They are rooted in the way the brain actually works: a personal judgement that is detrimental to objectivity and rationality. Humans have a cognitive and social bias to deny change and cast reality in familiar categories, which impairs the ability to see beyond existing systems and thinking patterns. There are said to be more than 180 cognitive biases; five relate specifically to the challenge of using the future for decision-making.

- Status quo bias: "change is bad for business". Predisposes you to ignore the facts because the alternatives are not desirable for you.
- Confirmation bias: interpreting information in a way that justifies existing beliefs. Extremely strong on issues a decision-maker is emotionally invested in; we quickly form an impression and then spend most of our time confirming it instead of collecting evidence.
- The expert problem: the more expert you are in a field, the less likely you are to see solutions at the fringes, outside the framework you were taught to think in.
- Availability heuristic: over-relying on the most readily available information or recent experiences, so that recent events disproportionately shape how future developments are evaluated.
- Groupthink: "how convenient that we all think the same". A lack of diversity in decision-making leads to inferior decisions; people who look alike and think alike are more likely to reach a consensus based on uninformed and misguided thinking.

Getting diverse thinking into a process is a mindset issue more than a tools issue. Being aware of your own and your group's biases is the place to start, remembering that foresight should be approached as a form of collective intelligence: quality outcomes can only be generated and assessed through dialogue between people bringing diverse perspectives, as non-partisan as possible.
