# Futures friction

Will the formats, products or services you are investing your time and resources in still be relevant in a few years? The assumptions behind our choices often seem so obvious that we rarely question whether they still hold. If media organisations only make room for ideas that fit today's strategy, they risk rejecting the very ideas that could keep them relevant as the surrounding system changes. Sofie Hvitved's two connected questions: how do we make better use of our collective organisational memory, and how do we keep questioning what we know as the logics around us change?

## What it is

- Futures friction brings futures thinking and foresight into the innovation process. It introduces deliberate friction to test ideas against possible futures, considering changes that might happen in both the short and the long term.
- A convincing AI-generated answer can make an idea feel settled before other possibilities have been explored: the risk of premature convergence. Intentional futures friction challenges the assumptions and biases behind that sense of certainty, making room to stop, think and iterate.
- It builds on a futurist's way of thinking: the conditions we work within could develop in several plausible directions, and the task is to explore what those differences would mean for the decisions made today. That means looking beyond short-term strategies and attending to signals that could challenge what is taken for granted. Some changes are incremental; others could fundamentally alter how people access information or whom they trust, which are existential questions for media; many needs and behaviours may endure.

## The questions it asks

- What would need to remain true for an idea to make sense?
- Under what conditions might an overlooked idea become relevant?
- If this kind of information becomes widely available, what would make our own service useful?

## How it works with shared context

- Shared context and futures friction need each other. A second brain turns shared context into infrastructure for remembering and learning; futures friction brings questioning into that infrastructure, helping organisations explore possible futures and recognise what they may need to reimagine.
- It means staying willing to change our minds as we learn. Short cycles of testing and reflection explore the questions in ongoing innovation work, and each experiment should add to the context others can draw on.
- In Hvitved's imagined 2030 team, friction enters once the earlier experiment has been understood: would the idea make sense if residents increasingly expected information to reach them through their own AI assistants? An agent helps explore that alongside the alternative, that people resist AI mediation and want direct contact with local journalists, and observations residents have agreed to share give reasons to take both seriously. The team stress-tests several ways of accessing the service; its purpose stays the same while the assumptions about how to fulfil it stay open. When a competitor launches a similar service weeks later, the team revisits its assumptions, adjusts the experiment and records what changed and why.

## Where to start

- Bring futures friction into the work from the beginning. Ask what must remain true for the idea to make sense. Test it against contrasting plausible futures, including one where the underlying media logics change.
- Choose a small experiment and agree what you need to learn, so that today's performance metrics do not dismiss an idea before its future value can be explored.
- Record what changes in your thinking, and give someone responsibility for revisiting the assumptions.
- Before returning to quarterly goals, consider which ideas never make it onto the agenda because they challenge the current logic. Give one of them time, resources and the benefit of what others have already learned, then use futures friction to explore where it could become relevant.

## Key things to consider

- This takes practice, from organising shared context to questioning what AI retrieves and thinking in futures. Leadership needs to protect time for it, recognise contributions across teams and allow learning to change priorities; a second brain is of little use if people are rewarded for holding knowledge back or the organisation refuses to reconsider its direction.
- It connects to the call for existential innovation, rethinking how media organisations sustain themselves and what they exist to do. Shared context and futures friction could create the conditions for that work: learning across teams, questioning inherited assumptions, and giving unfamiliar ideas room to develop.
- A futures-driven media industry begins with the decision to take an idea seriously before it fits today's strategic environment.
