# Foresight set-up archetypes

Strategic foresight as a formal organisational activity is relatively new in businesses and public organisations, and not as commonly practised as one would think, though it is rapidly emerging as an essential complement to conventional strategic thinking. Many functions and people work with the future in some capacity, often out of insights or innovation departments, but very often it is not formalised. Five archetypes represent the most common forms in which foresight practice is found.

- The lone wolves. Just about every organisation has some people interested in foresight and futures thinking, even if they are not tasked with it. Some have a background in futures thinking, but if their organisation does not value foresight they tend to keep quiet and find ways to smuggle foresight into their work. They may have an official job in design, innovation or consumer insights, which is inherently futures-focused, and they often want to expand their networks and join conversations about the future outside their organisation.
- The innovation or insights team. Its main task is to find opportunities on the horizon and at the fringes.
- The thought leaders. They focus mostly on developing big, bold, captivating narratives about the future. They may not always prioritise the details of strategic foresight as an integrated organisational activity, but they recognise its power in setting the agenda for their organisation and influencing the direction of their domain or industry, and many work to ensure their narrative is reflected in the organisation's strategy.
- The foresight-focused strategic planners. They work to enhance the planning and strategy functions by using foresight to extend planning horizons, and may be responsible for providing the insights leadership needs to evaluate a future contextual environment that may differ significantly from today. They hold the potential for building a discipline of foresight and anticipation by working systematically with the future, through environmental scanning, scenario planning or other approaches.
- The resident futurist. In some organisations formal foresight capacity lies in a single individual, empowered to provide foresight 'on demand' across the organisation. They often function as a trusted adviser, capable of reading, navigating and anticipating change, with a strong focus on translating and filtering foresight into strategy. Without an internal team, they often draw research from external sources or bring in other foresight professionals for specific tasks; the resident futurist may not even be an employee, but an external practitioner with an ongoing relationship.
