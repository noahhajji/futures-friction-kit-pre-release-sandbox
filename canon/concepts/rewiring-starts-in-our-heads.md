# Rewiring starts in our heads

Asked to imagine a newsroom in 2036, most people picture today's newsroom with some future sprinkled on top. We are conditioned to think in certain ways, so a reimagined thing can look new on the outside while the core stays unchanged. Sara Inkeri Vardar's conclusion, after five drafts of trying to come up with a solution, is that what journalism needs most may not be another brilliant idea but the curiosity to question the assumptions that shape the ideas in the first place.

## Are we solving the right problems?

- The crisis facing journalism is not necessarily experienced as a crisis by the public. People get on with their lives, find information elsewhere and do not feel anything is missing.
- When young people do not read the articles, the discussion turns to shorter articles, video or a new feed: new ways to deliver traditional journalism, without understanding what people are looking for. What if the problem is not the format, but information needs not met, barriers not noticed, questions not asked?
- Journalism's democratic responsibility goes beyond giving people what they ask for, but that should not make it less curious about the people it serves. How many product ideas begin with a genuine understanding of needs, and how many with an assumption about what will make people consume more of what is already produced?

## The value of a stupid question

- A proposal to let readers adjust the complexity level of an article with AI met immediate and legitimate objections: nuance, meaning, editorial responsibility. The proposer left feeling she had suggested something against the principles of journalism, when she was trying to make it accessible.
- Accuracy, nuance and responsibility are not details to ignore, but making journalism accessible to people who struggle to understand it is a journalistic ambition too. Journalists can protect editorial integrity so hard that they dismiss possibilities too quickly; developers can focus so hard on what is possible that they underestimate the consequences. Neither should end the conversation, and somewhere among the reasonable concerns and the hesitation to sound stupid, the user is forgotten.

## The burden of innovation

- Thinking differently is exhausting. Questioning the assumptions behind the work means giving up the certainty of knowing how it works and what the outcome will be, and doing so on top of deadlines.
- Research into innovation in established Nordic media organisations found the problem was rarely a lack of ideas or ambition but the difficulty of creating the time, resources and authority to act on them. When new initiatives compete with everyday operations, operations win: existing products have customers, deadlines and measurable results; new ideas come with assumptions, not KPIs. People are asked to create something unpredictable while being judged by how predictably they deliver.
- To do more than improve what exists, protect time and resources to question assumptions and to experiment with ideas whose outcomes cannot be predicted. Even if they never lead to a product, they might change how journalism is understood.

## Soft skills, really?

- Ideas never shared, questions never asked for fear of sounding stupid or because everyone else seems to know better. Creating the safety to share an idea before it is fully formed is among a leader's most important responsibilities.
- These are dismissed as soft skills, yet the ability to ask a question without knowing the answer, disagree with someone more experienced or share an unfinished idea decides which possibilities an organisation ever explores.
- It is also about giving up power: who decides what is worth pursuing, who controls the resources, and who is willing to give up some of that control. The paradox is that the more pressure the industry faces, the more it needs to innovate, and the more protective it becomes of resources, roles and influence.

## The newsroom of 2036

- Teams of journalists, developers, designers and people from backgrounds not traditionally associated with journalism, with the time, resources and authority to investigate problems together: not handed a product brief with a predefined solution, but encouraged to explore questions the organisation has not yet answered, with the people it is trying to reach involved in the exploration rather than invited in at the end to test what was already decided.
- Different pathways into journalism for people who do not look, think or work like those already inside, rather than diverse talent hired into the same structures. If you want the status quo questioned, it is counterproductive to recruit only people trained to see the world the same way.
- Organisations honest about what makes this difficult: the fear of being wrong, the tendency to protect what we have, and the power we might have to give up.
