# Public scepticism of AI in news

Findings from online surveys of nationally representative samples of adults, conducted in 2025 across mostly high-income countries in Europe, North America and Asia, as summarised by Rasmus Kleis Nielsen. They will not represent every country or every community.

## What people expect of AI-made news

- Asked whether news produced mostly by artificial intelligence with some human oversight would be more or less of various qualities than news produced entirely by human journalists, more respondents say it will be more up to date, easier to understand and informative than say the opposite.
- There are significantly more pessimists than optimists on whether such news will be transparent and, crucially, trustworthy.
- The quality most people expect of it: cheaper to make.

## What people assume news media already do

- A majority answer that news media use generative AI "always" or "often", a rate behind only search engine and social media companies.
- So whether or not a given journalist or publisher uses AI, much of the public is likely to assume they do, and its beliefs about what that means, less transparent, less trustworthy, cheaper, are likely to be priced in either way.

## Not a general crisis of trust

- Trust in news media is in long-term decline in much of the world, as it is for governments and political parties, but trust in the civil service, the legal system and science has held up or increased in many countries over the same period.
- Asked how generative AI will change their experience of various sectors, optimists outnumber pessimists for most sectors, except news media, governments and political parties. The contrast between optimism about search engine and even social media companies and pessimism about news media is particularly striking.

## Context

- Generative AI use is growing roughly twice as fast as internet use did in the 1990s and early 2000s, and many users find a fair share of synthetic content useful and valuable.
- Nielsen's reading: many kinds of content are likely to grow ever more commoditised, but relations are not, so the relationship between journalism and the public it claims to serve will only grow more important.
