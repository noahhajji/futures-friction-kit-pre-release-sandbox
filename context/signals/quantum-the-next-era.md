# Quantum – the next era

Quantum is a continuing signal that needs attention. In 2026, most organisations do not need a quantum strategy. It is simply too hard to act upon. But large organisations should now consider creating a quantum taskforce, or assigning clear ownership of the topic across strategy, innovation, cybersecurity, research or foresight. The exact setup is less important than the fact that quantum now deserves structured attention. It should be on the radar. Quantum has the potential to shift the technological ground beneath entire industries and societies.

## The harder challenge is imagination

- The central challenge is not only whether quantum will work, or when it will mature. The harder challenge is whether organisations can even imagine the reality it may unlock.
- Most firms are still asking whether quantum will produce useful applications. That is too limited a question. The deeper issue is whether leaders are prepared for a world in which the limits of computation, simulation and scientific discovery move beyond today's imagination. If that happens, the impact will not be confined to R&D teams. It could radically change how firms create value, where advantage sits, and which capabilities begin to count.
- A useful comparison is the printing press. It did not simply improve communication. It altered the infrastructure of knowledge, reduced the cost of coordination, widened access to information, and helped create new centres of power. Quantum could have a similarly structural effect in areas where current computational limits shape what can be invented, secured or understood. If practical thresholds are crossed, advantage may shift towards organisations able to use quantum to solve problems that others still cannot reach.

## Getting the timing right

- Many companies either dismiss quantum as too early, or wait until it becomes impossible to ignore.
- A better response is to build preparedness without getting pulled into hype: tracking technical progress, watching investment flows, paying attention to standards and regulation, and asking where quantum could intersect with the organisation's future business model, research agenda or security posture.
- The point is not to predict the exact moment of arrival. It is to avoid strategic surprise.

## Across the horizons

Quantum will look distant from Horizon 1, where near term delivery dominates. In Horizon 2, it becomes a question of literacy, scanning, preparedness and selective experimentation. In Horizon 3, it becomes a possible source of scientific acceleration, market reshuffling and strategic asymmetry. Quantum belongs here not as a near term certainty, but as a signal of a future shift that leaders cannot afford to notice too late.

## This includes

- Quantum computing
- Post quantum security
- Quantum enabled discovery
- Optimisation and simulation

## Questions

- Where could quantum alter the basis of value creation in our sector?
- Do we need a quantum taskforce, or another organisational mechanism, to monitor developments and translate them into strategic relevance?
- Are we prepared for a future in which the limits of modelling, discovery, and optimisation begin to shift?
- How exposed are our systems, data, and security assumptions to quantum enabled disruption?
- What would we need to see before quantum moves from a signal to active priority in our organisation?
