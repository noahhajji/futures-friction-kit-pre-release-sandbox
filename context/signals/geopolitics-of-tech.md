# The geopolitics of tech

One of the clearest geopolitical shifts is that, although technology was never neutral, this is now becoming far more visible.

## Technology as strategic infrastructure

- Technology is shaped by energy access and prices, water availability, semiconductor supply chains, rare earth mineral extraction, export controls and trade agreements. Access to AI depends not only on software and models, but on who can secure the physical and geopolitical conditions required to build and run them.
- The old image of software as borderless, frictionless and globally interchangeable is giving way to a more regionalised and contested infrastructure landscape, and awareness of who governs and programmes technologies from LLMs to electric vehicles. Resilience, localisation and strategic autonomy are becoming more important than cost optimisation alone, evident through a return to state industrial policy.
- Europe offers a clear signal. The EuroStack agenda argues for European capacity across the digital stack, from connectivity and cloud to AI and platforms. France has expanded sovereign state-operated tools such as Tchap and La Suite for public-sector communication and collaboration. The coming EU Tech Sovereignty Package is expected to push further on cloud, chips, open source and AI infrastructure. Geopolitical risk, trade pressure and concern over dependence on foreign providers are changing the norms around who should own, operate and control critical digital systems.

## What it means for organisations

- Infrastructure choices can no longer be treated as purely technical or financial decisions. Dependencies that once looked efficient may become strategic vulnerabilities. Supply chains can fragment. Regulation can diverge. Access to markets may be given, but often constrained by rivalry between states, blocs and standards regimes.
- Power is unevenly distributed across the technology stack. Some actors shape the terms of access to compute, chips, platforms and cloud infrastructure. Others remain downstream, dependent on systems they do not control. Apparently technical questions become contentious: who controls the mining and processing of rare earth minerals, who can manufacture advanced AI chips at scale, who can secure the energy and water required for advanced data centres, and who controls the infrastructure in space critical for connectivity. As AI becomes more economically significant, these are emerging as levers of power in their own right. They shape what organisations can build, buy, integrate and rely on.
- Preparedness requires an understanding of contemporary infrastructural dependencies and the geopolitical order these rely upon, a stronger scenario awareness, and a willingness to always think politically about technology.

## The AGI frontier

- Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) refers to systems capable of performing any intellectual task a human can, and potentially far beyond. The race towards achieving such general-purpose frontier systems is inherently geopolitical.
- If AGI-level systems were developed by a small number of firms or states, the consequences could extend well beyond productivity gains: a system capable of autonomously running scientific research, drafting legislation, managing critical infrastructure, or conducting cyber operations at a speed and scale no human institution could match or monitor. Whichever actors controlled such a system would hold an asymmetric advantage in economic output, military capability, and the ability to shape information at scale. The resulting concentration of power could prove extremely difficult to govern and potentially destabilise the wider international order. The question is not only about the capability of such systems, but who, if anyone, is in control.

## Across the horizons

This shift begins as a question of supply chain risk and cost, as organisations become more aware of how dependence on external infrastructures can create strategic vulnerability. In the long term, a fragmented technological order may force organisations and governments to rethink sourcing, ownership, and the conditions for staying adaptive and competitive.

## Questions

- Which technological dependencies in our organisation could become strategic vulnerabilities under geopolitical stress?
- How should we think about sovereignty and autonomy in relation to our own data, infrastructure, and suppliers?
- What scenarios could materially change our access to key technologies, markets, or partners over the next few years?
- How do regulatory divergence and strategic rivalry affect our long-term innovation choices today?
- What capabilities do we need to stay adaptive if the technological landscape fragments further along geopolitical lines?
