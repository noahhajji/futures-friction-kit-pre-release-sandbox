# Platforms move from distribution to content

Social media and other platforms, often owned by tech giants, have been dominant for a number of years. But for the first time ever, more people now get their news directly from platforms, not from news media. If people increasingly get their knowledge, information and news directly via AI, it can remove news media's control over their own content and their invaluable direct relationship to users. It moves power over information, and over journalism, away from news media and towards the tech companies that own the AI entry points.

Earlier, tech platforms largely outcompeted news media on distribution and advertising. Now they are going after taking over the content as well. That hits not only the business foundation but the very influence and relevance of news media.

## The access model shakes

The dominant or growing information entry points, platforms and increasingly AI entry points, are controlled and developed by foreign tech companies. Even where media are strong, that challenges the direct relationship between the news medium's brand and journalism and the user. It shakes what can be called journalism's access model. Publishers are losing control over their content, and whoever controls the assembly of information has the power; no news media have that control. The result can be their gradually disappearing relevance. If the front page dies, where does the medium's best content live? Does it become interfaces supported by new AI devices? And does the future of media become to be content factories for the entry points that users find give the greatest value?

## What it calls for

In the short term, collaboration between media can concern securing real payment for the content used in AI products, possibly via a form of subscription; securing visibility; letting people move easily on to the medium itself; and keeping a degree of control over the content. Alliances between large news organisations and licensing initiatives are already under way. They are decisive for a certain negotiating position towards the tech companies and for innovating towards systems and business models in which news media can also thrive in the new information ecosystem. It may also indicate that the AI companies need some of the journalistic content: AI platforms increasingly rely on trusted news content to understand and respond to real-time events, and agreements giving AI assistants access to news in real time have been seen as a structural shift in the AI ecosystem.

In the longer term the need for collaboration goes beyond licensing agreements. Are news media merely to be content factories, or can they build a new trusted layer: new interfaces, or layers, that people will use, also as an alternative to AI products and platforms? That will require large, broad collaborations, and it concerns not only the existence and business models of media. Who owns the information infrastructure is a core democratic question. It may call for collaboration with legislators and governments, and with other institutions in society within research, financing, technology and education. Such collaborations will require many difficult trade-offs, not least regarding the independence of news media and their classic internal competition.
