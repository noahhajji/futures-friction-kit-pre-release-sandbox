# Shifting world order

## What it is

The structural erosion of the US-led unipolar order and its replacement by a multipolar configuration means that no single power holds uncontested authority over the rules, institutions and norms governing international affairs. The rise of China takes centre stage in the reconfiguration of the geopolitical arena, but an equally important part of the story is played by middle powers with newfound autonomy and diverging interests.

Since the end of the Cold War, American power, military, economic, cultural and technological, has dominated international affairs. That era of near-uncontested hegemony has ended. Different concepts and frameworks have been proposed to help understand the transition period where no clear successor architecture to the Western-led order has yet crystallised, including 'multipolarity' and 'multiplex world'. No consensus has so far emerged, perhaps owing to the uncertainties around how exactly this new constellation will look, what the relative power balance between states will be, and which institutions, if any, will govern.

## Sub-trends

- From alliances to pragmatic and transactional bonds
- Middle powers multi-aligning
- The rise of China
- Breakdown in governing mythologies (e.g. the rules-based order)
- Rearmament and global growth in military spending
- Technological leadership rivalry
- Old institutional order challenged

## Uncertainties

- Which new governing mythologies will become dominant after the transition?
- Will the Thucydides trap spring, leading to direct conflict between the US and China? (The Thucydides trap is the tendency towards war as an emerging power threatens to displace an existing great power.)
- How will the distribution of power between the world's multiple geopolitical poles be weighted?
- Will we see effective global standards and agreements to address cross-border issues, ranging from IP rights and standardisation to climate policy?
- Will democracy continue its global decline? If not, what might spark its revival?
- How will the declining legitimacy of Western institutions and frameworks affect the development paths of nations across the world?

## Drivers

- Some of the building blocks for this transition were laid decades ago. The early 21st century saw a high point in US imperial overreach with the invasion of Iraq without a UN mandate in 2003, after which the credibility of American global leadership and the rules-based order it championed began a steady decline.
- China's rise to an industrial, economic and military powerhouse, decades in the making, has facilitated its ability to mount a growing challenge to the Western-led order.
- Russia's newfound assertiveness came to a culmination with the full-scale war in Ukraine started in 2022, but its beginnings can be traced back to the incursion into Georgia in 2008 (and the preceding Munich conference in 2007) and the annexation of Crimea in 2014.
- In the West, the democratic decline witnessed over the last decade has eroded trust in the institutions established in the 20th century to provide the scaffolding of the post-WW2 world order.
- Taken together, what may have started as weaker signals of a potential transition have converged into the unravelling of the 'unipolar moment' that began at the end of the Cold War in 1991.
- Macroeconomic indicators support the shift. Measured across regions, the share of global GDP is amid a multi-decade shift from the Global North to China and emerging Asian economies. 'De-dollarisation', a reduction in the use of USD for international trade and financial transactions, is another signal that relative financial and geopolitical power is shifting away from the US, although this trend's future trajectory is highly contingent.
- Technological leadership is becoming a central axis of geopolitical competition, and China has emerged as a credible systemic contender. Over the past decade, China has moved beyond catch-up dynamics to establish leadership positions in artificial intelligence, clean energy technologies, advanced manufacturing and space capabilities, underpinned by state-led industrial policy, scale advantages in data and infrastructure, and an ability to mobilise capital and coordinate long-term strategic priorities. In solar photovoltaics, battery production and electric vehicles, China already holds dominant global market positions, while in AI and space it is rapidly narrowing gaps with the US. The EU is on a downward slope of relative innovation capacity in many of the same domains.
- China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) is both an economic and a geopolitical instrument. Through large-scale investments in infrastructure, energy and connectivity across Asia, Africa, Europe and Latin America, it has expanded China's economic footprint and deepened interdependencies with participating countries, and it facilitates the diffusion of standards, technologies and governance approaches aligned with Chinese interests. It reflects the emergence of alternative frameworks for global integration that operate alongside, or in competition with, Western-led institutions.
- The post-Cold War 'peace dividend', a relative decline in interstate conflict along with disarmament and reduced military expenditure across much of the West, enabled a reallocation of resources towards economic integration and domestic development. That period is now clearly ending.

## Manifestations

- Multi-alignment among middle powers. Türkiye, a NATO member since 1952, positioned at the intersection of Europe, the Middle East and Asia, operates within multiple overlapping spheres of influence, balancing long-standing ties with Western-led institutions alongside deepening economic, energy, defence and diplomatic relations with non-Western partners. Singapore hosts US military assets while refraining from directly supporting US goals in the region that run counter to Chinese ambitions, and participates in both China's BRI and the US-led Indo-Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity. India is simultaneously a member of the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue with the US, Japan and Australia, a founding BRICS state, a longtime buyer of Russian arms and (since 2022) discounted Russian oil, and a vocal advocate of strategic autonomy as an explicit doctrinal term. Such behaviour expresses a wider pattern of states hedging risks, diversifying dependencies and maintaining room for manoeuvre where global power is more diffused and less predictable. Multi-alignment is becoming a structural feature rather than a novel phenomenon.
- Growing geopolitical agency and confidence in the Global South, evidenced in the willingness of countries in Southeast Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East and parts of Latin America to resist alignment pressures, assert independent policy positions and demand greater representation in global governance institutions. Legitimacy is no longer conferred primarily by Western-led institutions but increasingly negotiated across a broader and more diverse set of actors, visible in UN General Assembly voting data where alignment of many Global South countries with Western goals is now less pronounced.
- Global democratic backsliding and the growing appeal of more autocratic forms of government, although whether this is a contributing cause, a symptom, or both, is an open question. According to the V-Dem Institute, the number of countries autocratising rose from 12 in 2004 to 45 in 2024, while countries democratising fell from 26 to 19. Features of strong liberal democracies like freedom of expression and quality of elections are on a global decline. The US is amid a decade-long democratic backsliding trend, with attacks on the judiciary, the abolition of accountability institutions, military purges and attacks on the freedom of the media as key contributors. Democratisation is highly historically contingent, its strongest waves flowing from changes to geopolitical power dynamics.
- The breakdown of the governing mythologies that have underpinned the international order for decades. One is the association between liberal democracy and 'the good life': while levels of democracy have decreased globally, the provision of public goods is on the rise across both illiberal and democratic states. Another is the 'rules-based international order', which some argue was always at least partly a mirage, a set of norms and institutions designed to provide legitimacy to powerful states acting out of self-interest. Recent events in Gaza, Ukraine and Iran, and the different responses to them, have illustrated that Western appeals to a 'rules-based order' differ from appeals to 'international law' as administered by the International Court of Justice and the International Criminal Court. The same US House of Representatives that welcomed ICC proceedings against Vladimir Putin voted in favour of the Illegitimate Court Counteraction Act to sanction ICC officials over arrest warrants for Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant. Invoking 'the rules' while applying them selectively deepens perceptions of Western hypocrisy. The legitimacy of supranational governance is also counteracted by China's position, which emphasises the primacy of consent and the supremacy of state sovereignty.
- The growing appeal of non-Western governance frameworks, such as BRICS and Global East forums and the Mecca Defence Alliance between Türkiye, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, constitutes an intensifying legitimacy contest over whose norms, institutions and governing mythologies get to define the international system.
- Military spending is rising globally, with significant increases across Europe, Asia and the Middle East, reflecting a renewed focus on deterrence, territorial defence and strategic competition.
- The nature of conflict is expanding beyond traditional domains to include cyber operations, economic coercion, weaponisation of migration and information, and technological rivalry. Space is emerging as a new frontier of great power contest, marked by growing militarisation and competition. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty faces growing strain as spacefaring nations prioritise military applications, and space is a site of competition for resources needed in such large quantities that transportation from Earth is not feasible. As national space agencies and private enterprises establish permanent settlements on the Moon, they may become increasingly controlling over access to certain natural resources found there.

## Implications

- These developments point to a structural transition away from an era defined by expectations of peace and towards one where conflict, both direct and indirect, is a more persistent feature of the international system.
- Conflict does not need to be the inevitable conclusion to a contested transition. The alternative is painful adjustments and negotiated accommodation by the world's new hegemons and the new cast of middle powers. Some will need to accept a diminished role while others must exercise restraint in their newfound influence and agency.
- Whether the right balance can be struck will be among the defining questions of the coming decades, shaped not only by Washington, Beijing and Moscow, but by the cumulative choices of the dozens of states now hedging between them.
- A key question is whether one set of institutions and narratives will dominate or whether the field will remain contested.
- Standards, platforms and innovation ecosystems may increasingly diverge along geopolitical lines as the technological order becomes more contested.

Three stacked line charts sharing a common time axis from 1990 to just past 2020, with a shared vertical axis labelled total FBIC. The United Arab Emirates rises from just above zero in the early 1990s, climbing steadily through the 2000s to roughly 6 by the early 2020s, the steepest of the three. Indonesia begins near 1 and rises only gently, to roughly 2-3 by the early 2020s. Türkiye begins near zero, rises through the 2000s to around 5 by 2015, then continues up to roughly 7-8 by the early 2020s. Formal Bilateral Influence Capacity measures the capacity for influence between pairs of states across economic, political and security relationships. By this measure, countries like the UAE, Indonesia and Türkiye have seen their influence in the international system increase since the end of the Cold War, demonstrating the growing agency of middle powers in a more fluid geopolitical environment.
