# Health beyond the clinic

## What it is

Health management is shifting from a matter of communicable disease and acute treatment to one of chronic, long-term management embedded in everyday life. A century ago, infectious disease dominated mortality. Today, non-communicable diseases account for most non-pandemic deaths. This reflects not only ageing, which means people live longer with chronic conditions, but also health outcomes being shaped by people's environment and circumstance well before they reach a doctor.

The future of health is marked by a series of challenges which include growing self-management (and the inequality in who can do it well), healthcare systems strained by long-term care, and an ageing population whose extra years do not necessarily translate proportionately into extra years of health. New tools like wearables and AI guidance offer partial help but create new risks around reliability, health literacy and trust. Whether interventions can close these gaps, and how, remains a central question.

## Sub-trends

- Rise of chronic diseases
- Health risks embedded in everyday systems
- Recognition and growth of mental health needs
- Expansion of self-managed healthcare
- Growth of digital health tools
- New actors and business models
- Inequality in access to care, health literacy and prevention

## Uncertainties

- Will government interventions in areas such as food security and housing quality mitigate health risks, or will structural drivers of chronic risk remain embedded in systems and infrastructures?
- How will misinformation and trust (or lack of it) in science-based health information affect attitudes towards public health interventions, medications and diagnostic tools?
- How will health innovation impact health systems and preventive models of care?
- Who will have the overview and ownership of all relevant individual health data? Will a balance be struck between data protection and privacy and interoperability of systems and institutions?
- Who will be held responsible when decision-support tools, AI-mediated or otherwise, produce adverse health outcomes or behaviour?
- Will health financing and incentive structures evolve to reward innovation, outcomes-based reimbursement for medical professionals, and preventive, healthier-living behaviours among individuals? Or will financing remain anchored to volume-based care and treatment of illness after the fact, leaving current incentive structures largely intact?

## Drivers

- A century ago, infectious diseases such as tuberculosis, influenza and pneumonia were the leading causes of death across much of the world, and only one in six deaths were due to a chronic condition. This began to change in the mid-twentieth century, when sanitation, clean water, vaccination programmes, and the widespread adoption of antibiotics and public health measures greatly extended life expectancy, allowing populations to live long enough to develop chronic conditions.
- Dietary shifts toward calorie-dense processed foods and increasingly sedentary lifestyles compounded the prevalence of these same chronic conditions. Rapid urbanisation (linked to poor diets, physical inactivity and air pollution), rising global obesity rates, tobacco use and increasingly sedentary jobs are more important contributors than longer lives alone.
- Health risks are increasingly embedded in everyday systems, like food environments, work life, transport patterns, urban environments and housing conditions. Health outcomes are often shaped long before people consider contacting their doctor.
- Because chronic disease develops slowly, today's prevalence figures reflect exposures accumulated across a generation rather than recent changes in behaviour. Annual per capita sales of ultra-processed foods rose 40% across lower-middle income countries between 2007 and 2022, and by as much as 60% in Uganda, while sales in high-income countries stayed flat. Today, 31% of adults worldwide do not meet minimum physical activity levels, projected to rise to 35% by 2030 if present trends hold.
- Pollution in rapidly developing urban areas is a tenacious cause of chronic illness. Air pollution in megacities such as Delhi, Dhaka and Lahore routinely exceeds WHO limits, sometimes more than tenfold. As climate change intensifies, extreme weather and its associated health risks become a permanent part of everyday life in large parts of the world, especially in Africa and Asia. Extreme heat significantly accelerates the progression of chronic kidney disease and can trigger acute cardiovascular problems.

## Manifestations

- Non-communicable diseases account for 75% of all non-pandemic deaths globally. The conditions driving this burden (heart disease, diabetes, cancer, chronic respiratory disease) are not resolved through a single treatment but are lived with and managed over years. Living with multiple chronic conditions at once has become much more common.
- An estimated 650 million people were living with cardiovascular disease in 2023, some 468 million with a chronic respiratory condition in 2021, and the International Diabetes Federation projects 853 million people living with diabetes by 2050, nearly one in ten adults alive today. Most will be managing their conditions at home or at work, through apps, medication adherence, dietary choices and daily decisions made without clinical support.
- 82% of premature deaths from non-communicable diseases occur in low- and middle-income countries, where prevention infrastructure is limited, diagnosis often comes late, and chronic disease compounds rather than replaces the existing infectious disease burden.
- Nearly one in seven people live with a mental disorder, most commonly anxiety and depression. Suicide is the third leading cause of death among people aged 15 to 29 worldwide, with men twice as affected as women, and 73% of the world's suicides happen in low- and middle-income countries. Mental disorders frequently co-occur with physical chronic conditions.
- Care increasingly relies on tasks that patients and their families carry out themselves. Individuals must navigate an immense amount of health information from clinical portals, apps and wearables, insurers, employers and social media, with varying degrees of reliability or alignment.
- Health literacy tracks the socioeconomic gradient. Those with more education, more time and more stable circumstances are best placed to engage with a system that requires self-management, while those with the least of these things are offered the least structured support. The democratisation of health technologies, and of AI-mediated health guidance in particular, has begun to narrow parts of this gap by making tailored information available to people who previously had no route to it. But the same tools deliver unverified or unverifiable information at scale, and the capacity to tell the difference is itself a function of health literacy.
- In nearly three-quarters of countries with data, medicines account for at least 55% of out-of-pocket spending, rising to a median of 60% among people living in poverty. In 2022, 2.1 billion people experienced financial hardship due to out-of-pocket health spending.
- Health wearables support continuous individualised monitoring of heart rate, nutrition, stress levels and sleep patterns. With AI-powered chatbots, individuals can seek guidance on any health-related question in seconds, though some researchers have found the error rate of AI-generated health answers to be twice as high as for human physicians. People's confidence in their own ability to make health decisions has fallen sharply, down ten points globally in a single year. The erosion of trust in scientific authority predates generative AI, which entered an information environment already shaped by social media and few trusted intermediaries between researchers and the public.
- The number of years people live in poor health (the morbidity gap) widened globally from 8.8 years in 1990 to 10.7 in 2023, with the largest gaps in the wealthiest countries, led by the United States, Australia and Canada. Those years are spread across adulthood, not only the end of life. 80% of long-term care in Europe is performed by informal caregivers, the majority women.
- Long-term care is the fastest-growing component of age-related public spending, projected to almost double across OECD countries by 2050 to 2.8% of GDP, and it is the component least amenable to productivity gains, since care requires human interaction.
- Assisted dying legislation has surfaced in many countries and is under active consideration across a widening set of European jurisdictions. The contested path of Portugal's 2023 law, adopted by parliament but held up by presidential veto and constitutional challenge, illustrates how unsettled the terrain remains.
- Some signals point to a healthier future. Tobacco use has fallen worldwide continuously over the last 20 years, although gains are partly offset by a shift to e-cigarettes, heated tobacco and nicotine pouches. Consumers in the US, UK, Germany and China rank wellness as a top or important priority, with mental and cognitive health among the areas of greatest unmet need. The global wellness economy has roughly doubled in value since 2013, though it remains concentrated in high-income markets.

## Implications

- Health management is becoming a permanent condition of life, stretching across childhood, education, work, family formation and old age.
- The health outcome of a worker in an Asian or African megacity is decided less by their genetic makeup or which communicable disease they may contract than by systemic factors: the city they live in, the air they breathe, the food they can access or afford, the way they must conduct their work, and more extreme weather. Health is a systemic challenge like no other and will need to be addressed in a systemic manner.
- For one group, the new health landscape means flexibility and autonomy. For the other, it is an added burden. More responsibility for health management may also mean higher costs for individuals and families.
- More input alone, from doctors, AI tools, social media creators and other sources, does not create a better or more empowering information environment.
- If medicine is increasingly capable of extending life without proportionately extending the quality of it, societies will have to decide what they are extending life for.
- A major question is how to close the gap between those who can manage their health proactively and those who cannot. If left unsolved, growing health inequality will become a defining feature of this century.
- Traditional healthcare systems will likely remain the bedrock of health, but they may only handle a fraction of what health management will entail over the coming decades. The rest will be distributed across everyday life: homes, workplaces, devices, family and friends.
