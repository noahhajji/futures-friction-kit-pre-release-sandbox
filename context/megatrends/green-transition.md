# The green transition

## What it is

Climate change, ecological degradation and breached planetary boundaries are forcing a shift away from the fossil-fuel-based, resource-intensive economy of the 20th century. That shift is increasingly reinforced by economics, as renewables are quickly becoming the cheaper option in many markets. The Green Transition encompasses both the shift to renewable energy and efforts to halt the environmental degradation driven by resource-intensive production.

This transition is underway, but it proceeds unevenly, both in terms of geographies and efforts. Renewable energy capacity is expanding, and CO2 emissions are measurably lower than they would otherwise be, while progress on protecting natural habitats and reducing material consumption lags further behind. Political backlash against sustainability agendas in several major economies creates noise and distracts attention, but it has not reversed the trajectory of the megatrend. The visible pressures continue to build, but these dynamics alone are no guarantee that the transition will happen fast enough to avert severely disruptive climate risks.

Unlike most megatrends, which concern human actions in the social system, the Green Transition occurs in a complex physical system with feedback loops and system thresholds that can produce sudden reversals in indicators. Human action can improve a wide range of parameters, such as the net forest area, but physical mechanisms such as the die-off in the Amazon could still be triggered, fundamentally disrupting the indicator being improved.

## Sub-trends

- Energy transition from fossil fuels to renewables
- Circular economy and sustainable resource use
- Electrification
- Increase in policies to prevent environmental degradation
- Increase in nature-related financial disclosures
- Growing climate mitigation and regulation efforts
- New regions and countries stepping up in climate leadership

## Uncertainties

- What will be the duration and impact of the current backlash against renewables?
- Will new energy storage technologies achieve major gains in efficiency and propel the pace of electrification and renewable energy use?
- What would it take for consumer awareness to translate into a widespread shift in consumer choices?
- Will Earth system tipping points be reached, resulting in runaway climate calamities, before the transition can prevent them?
- Will the restoration of natural habitats keep in step with the energy transition, or will biodiversity loss continue to worsen?
- Will the world unite around climate action, or will efforts be regionalised?

## Drivers

- For most of the past century, economic growth came at a measurable cost to the natural world. Livestock now outweigh wild mammals and birds tenfold, air pollution accounts for approximately seven million premature deaths each year, and climate change proceeds at a pace that inches closer to dangerous tipping points in the planet's climate systems.
- As climate change accelerates, the physical impacts in the form of severe weather events and ecosystem collapse place increasing adaptation demands on societies. The need for robust, science-based tools to anticipate risk, manage uncertainty and design resilient systems will grow over the long term, regardless of short-term political cycles. The transition is not only an environmental necessity but increasingly an economic and strategic one.
- Economics reinforce the shift. Global clean energy investment stands at USD 1.8 trillion annually, against USD 1 trillion in fossil fuels. Solar paired with 4-hour battery storage is now cheaper than new coal generation in major markets.
- China's scale and policy direction make it a deciding factor in the global outcome. China leads the world in installed capacity of both solar and wind power and accounts for a substantial share of new additions each year. Massive investments in utility-scale solar farms and offshore wind have driven down costs globally through economies of scale and manufacturing dominance, and grid modernisation, including ultra-high-voltage transmission lines, enables renewable energy generated in remote regions to reach dense urban centres.
- The evolving climate risk landscape pushes in the direction of a transition. As carbon is increasingly priced into major markets and institutional investors are required to account for climate risk, the economics are shifting in ways that reach beyond the energy sector. Decisions once made purely on cost and convenience now increasingly involve assessing climate risk and meeting new and stronger regulations.
- International environmental governance is expanding. The High Seas Treaty in 2025 marks a significant step toward protecting marine ecosystems in international waters. The UN Convention to Combat Desertification has gained renewed momentum, with parties pledging to restore one billion hectares of degraded land by 2030. Trade in endangered species is being restricted.

## Manifestations

- The overall picture is one of mixed signals. The global deforestation rate is slowing (from 17.6 million hectares per year in 1990-2000 to 10.9 million in 2015-2025). The average proportion of key biodiversity areas under protection has increased from roughly 25% in 2000 to about 45% in 2025. The share of renewables in the global energy mix is growing steadily. Yet the Red List Index, an indicator of overall extinction risk, has been continuously worsening for decades, and seven of Earth's nine planetary boundaries are now breached.
- The energy transition is the part of the megatrend furthest along. Across major economies there has been a decoupling of GDP growth and CO2 emissions. Renewables account for a relatively small share of primary energy consumption, but that figure conceals the higher efficiency of electricity: around 40% less renewable energy than fossil fuel energy is needed to perform the same task, so the actual substitution share of renewables is higher and rising quickly.
- Electrification of transport is a critical trend. The electric vehicle share of global car sales stands at 25% in 2026 and is rising quickly. In Norway, EVs' share of new passenger vehicle sales reached 96% in 2025.
- The transition is not fast enough. National emissions plans are expected to cut emissions by 12% in the next 10 years, which is not sufficient to curb accelerating climate change. The Paris Agreement aimed to limit warming to well below 2°C with efforts to cap it at 1.5°C, yet the world is on track for 2.6°C. The planet is already 1.41°C warmer than pre-industrial levels, and the Copernicus Climate Change Service projects a breach of 1.5°C as early as May 2029 rather than March 2042.
- Restoration and protection of natural habitats lag behind the energy transition for several reasons. Nature preservation efforts do not scale in the same way renewable energy does. Institutional frameworks are at an earlier stage than climate mitigation. Protecting natural environments often means not doing something, or forgoing short-term economic gain, which is harder to incentivise. And since industrialised agriculture and food production are the leading cause of both deforestation and biodiversity loss, addressing root causes means reforming the very systems that sustain a growing global population and on which the livelihoods of billions depend.
- Businesses face regulatory pressure in the form of carbon pricing mechanisms and supply chain due diligence laws. Companies that once treated sustainability as a voluntary commitment now navigate it as a compliance obligation, and the pace of regulatory change is itself a challenge. The EU's Right to Repair Directive (adopted 2024, applicable from 2026) and the Ecodesign Regulation for Sustainable Products (2024) aim to combat planned obsolescence.
- Consumer uptake is slow. Surveys consistently show that large majorities consider climate change a serious threat, yet this concern rarely translates into proportionate changes in behaviour. Most people still fly, consume and buy as many products as they can afford, motivated by convenience and comfort more than ethical considerations. This gap between values and behaviour is one of the defining tensions of the Green Transition. The consumer society is also a product of deliberate psychological and cultural pressures, as industries engineered products and marketing to exploit reward circuits while eroding the expectation of quality.
- Public resistance accompanies change on this scale. The costs of the transition are sometimes immediately felt as higher fuel prices, restricted choices or disrupted livelihoods, while the benefits feel distant or abstract, so political backlash often follows. Farmers block highways, yellow vests flood the streets, lobbyists roll up their sleeves, and governments that moved ambitiously on climate policy walk back commitments under electoral pressure.

## Implications

- Humanity's shift away from a resource-intensive economy is an accelerating trend, unlikely to be reversed by short-term political backlash, but it does not guarantee arrival at a stable end state. The transition is to a future in which we will likely be forced to mitigate, and adapt to, climate change and environmental degradation simultaneously.
- The climate is approaching a high risk of Earth system tipping points such as the polar ice sheets melting, permafrost thawing and warm-water coral reefs dying, which would be catastrophic and irreversible. Modestly reduced emissions curves are not likely to prevent one or more of these from being reached. Planning on the assumption of an intact climate system is no longer prudent. Every tenth of a degree still counts, and so does the possibility that some thresholds are passed anyway.
- The societal and economic knock-on effects are already evident. Clean energy jobs surpassed those from fossil fuels for the first time in 2021, and the clean energy sector accounted for 10% of global GDP growth in 2023, including 20% of China's and nearly one third of the EU's.
- A 'degrowth' future may be doubtful, but a forced re-emphasis on quality over volume is quite possible.
- Backlash will not reverse the transition, but it will continue to shape how it proceeds: slowly, unevenly, and through periods of contest and friction.
