# The constrained economy

## What it is

The global economy is undergoing a structural shift from the expansionary configuration of the post-Cold War era to a more constrained one. Many of the enabling conditions that drove growth, integration and convergence for three decades have either exhausted themselves or gone into reverse. Lower growth is one consequence of this shift, but the deeper change is the constraints and conditions under which states, companies and individuals now pursue economic activity.

The story of the global economy in the late 20th and early 21st century has been one of convergence and expansion: rising international trade, aided by deeper multilateral engagement and developing nations opening to trade, and the emergence of a truly global economic system following China's opening (late 1970s) and the USSR's collapse (1991), alongside the proliferation of bilateral trade and FDI deals. Underpinning this was a unique set of monetary conditions, dollar hegemony and cheap capital, combined with falling marginal tax rates, privatisation and deregulation. Industrial policy was treated as a controversial, even taboo, topic. The global economy is now entering a new phase defined less by the structures that enabled its expansion and more by those that constrain it.

## Sub-trends

- Rising fiscal and private financial stress
- Constrained global growth
- Fading demographic tailwinds leading to shrinking labour supply
- Geopolitical uncertainty
- Rise in trade and supply chain disruptions
- Growing resource constraints

## Uncertainties

- Will rising public debt and underfunded pension systems constrain fiscal space and slow long-term growth, particularly in ageing economies and highly indebted countries?
- How will geopolitical tensions and shifting trade and industrial policies reshape global trade, investment flows and the resilience of the global economy to external shocks?
- How large will the economic costs of climate mitigation and adaptation be?
- Will financial innovation outside the regulated banking system strengthen economic dynamism or create new systemic risks and financial instability?
- To what extent will the expansion of AI lead to productivity increases, and where in the economy?
- Can ageing economies reconcile their need for migrants and talent with rising nationalist and anti-immigration politics?

## Drivers

- Many of the conditions that made the post-1990 growth era possible are exhausted. As the demographic tailwinds of the 20th century fade in many regions, labour supply growth is reduced. In the OECD's long-run scenarios, global annual potential output growth is projected to moderate from around 2.9% today to 2.7% in the early 2030s and 2.1% in the early 2040s, reflecting declining working-age population growth and a slowing trend in labour productivity growth in emerging market economies.
- Investment has weakened, feeding directly into slower productivity growth and lower output growth. Trade has lost momentum as policy uncertainty rises and fragmentation increases trade costs.
- Debt is a further constraint. High levels of debt have translated into high debt service burdens. Over the last decade, an increasing number of low-income economies spend more on servicing public debt than on health, education and public investment. Higher interest rates amplify this pressure by raising refinancing costs and narrowing fiscal space for infrastructure, education and industrial policy.
- The distribution of gains from growth remains highly uneven. The key dynamic in the past couple of years has been an increase in the share of wealth of the 0.01% richest people globally. Such concentration shapes consumption patterns, investment allocation and political stability, even when headline GDP growth remains positive.
- Geopolitical conflict and war have become a more visible economic constraint. Beyond the human cost, conflict increases risk premia, disrupts supply chains, raises energy and food price volatility and accelerates strategic decoupling. With tensions at their highest since the end of the Cold War, the resulting uncertainty depresses investment and trade and can lock economies into lower growth paths. Rising defence spending draws resources away from budgets without the same long-term productive return as other public investment.
- The structure and complexity of global finance is evolving. The US dollar remains the dominant reserve currency, but official central bank reserves have slowly diversified toward non-traditional reserve currencies such as Australian dollars. Shifts in reserve composition influence liquidity conditions, capital flows and the resilience of the international monetary system in periods of stress.
- Resource constraints are more pressing. Money cannot simply buy access to what is scarce or politically restricted. Inputs such as lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper and graphite are becoming strategically important for electrification and advanced manufacturing, with rising demand and growing supply chain concentration risks. If access becomes a binding constraint, it can reshape industrial competitiveness and slow the transition investments that many growth strategies rely on.

## Manifestations

- Lower growth rates are the most visible symptom of the structural shift, but they are a consequence rather than the story itself. Global growth slowed from an average of 3.6% per year in the decade before the Global Financial Crisis (1998-2008) to 3.2% in the decade before the Covid pandemic (2010-2020). Restoring growth to earlier decade averages will require a very large collective policy effort, including stronger cooperation, larger financing and mobilising private capital.
- In the long term, weak growth may be offset by AI, primarily through productivity gains. The IMF describes AI as a potential force that could jumpstart productivity, boost global growth and raise incomes, while warning that benefits may be uneven and could deepen inequality without policy responses. Goldman Sachs Research estimates that generative AI could raise global GDP by 7% over ten years and lift productivity growth by 1.5 percentage points per year, conditional on adoption and task exposure. The OECD frames AI as a general-purpose technology with the potential to influence productivity, distribution and growth, while emphasising uncertainty around macro outcomes and diffusion beyond leading firms.
- Regardless of whether AI eventually offsets weaker growth rates, the underlying structural reconfiguration of labour, capital, trade, debt and resource conditions is more persistent. An economy that grows at 3% through AI-driven productivity gains while operating under demographic constraint, fragmented trade and resource scarcity is structurally different from one that grew at 3.6% through demographic expansion, open trade and cheap energy. Growth rates can recover while the structure underneath them, debt capacity, labour supply, trade openness and input access, remains permanently more constrained.

## Implications

- Nations and companies will increasingly pay a premium for security in the form of redundant supply chains, strategic reserves, domestic capacity and trusted partners. What once looked inefficient may now become economically rational.
- Governments will face harder choices on defence, climate, welfare, pensions and more, in an environment where the economic pie expands more slowly and prioritisation matters more.
- With shrinking domestic labour forces, productivity becomes even more important. AI, automation and organisational innovation become strategic imperatives, while people face growing pressure to continuously adapt their skills.
- A smaller workforce will be asked to finance larger ageing populations, forcing difficult choices on pensions, healthcare and taxation. For younger people, the implicit promise that each generation will become wealthier than the last could become harder to sustain.
- Heightened competition for migrants and talent may be expected globally. Individuals with scarce skills gain bargaining power, while others face greater pressure to reskill.
- Uncertainties abound. Redundant supply chains, strategic reserves and trusted-partner sourcing come at a cost that becomes difficult to sustain amid the rising fiscal and financial stress driving the trend in the first place; resilience may become one of the first casualties when governments and firms are forced to economise. Whether competition for migrants and talent materialises as open competition, or whether nationalist and anti-immigration politics in ageing economies foreclose migration as a lever, is a crucial question. And even in an optimistic scenario, it remains open whether AI-driven productivity growth arrives quickly and broadly enough to ease the financing burden on ageing populations, or stays concentrated in leading firms and high-skill workers, doing little to relieve the distributive strain.
