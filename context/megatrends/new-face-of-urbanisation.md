# The new face of urbanisation

## What it is

Over the course of this century, urban milieus, primarily in Asia and Africa, will undergo developmental changes with unprecedented speed and complexity. The new urban epicentres will expand faster than institutions can adapt, often at lower income levels, and under demographic, climate and governance pressures that make comparison to any previous wave of city-building difficult. The central question is not only how many people will live in cities, but also what kinds of urban worlds will emerge and who will be included in them.

Cities have always been where human civilisation experiments on itself. Novel ideas emerge there, and radical solutions often get tested there first. They are microcosms of the plurality of futures ahead. More than half of the world's population currently lives in urban areas, and by 2050 this share is projected to rise to 68% (6.6 billion people). Asia and Africa will host around 90% of the additional urban population in the next three decades.

## Sub-trends

- Ascension of megacities (10+ million residents)
- Urbanisation happening earlier in economic development
- Growing prevalence of informal housing
- Housing affordability pressures
- Rural-to-urban influx
- Forced migration and displacement

## Uncertainties

- Will governance be able to adapt in the fastest urbanising regions to prevent entrenched poverty at scale?
- What will be the balance between public and private ownership and governance of the urban infrastructure?
- To what extent will climate and environmental constraints disrupt urban growth trajectories?
- Will urban concentration generate broad-based prosperity or deepen inequality between formal and informal zones?
- Will the new megacities leapfrog to design paradigms suited to emerging needs, or will they be built on models designed for a world that no longer exists?

## Drivers

- Historically, cities have been associated with economic expansion and rising incomes, but the relationship is not straightforward. What makes the current wave historically distinct is the income level at which it is happening. Sub-Saharan Africa and much of South Asia are urbanising far earlier in their economic development than Latin America or East Asia did. These regions cannot always supply the infrastructure, institutions and services that dense urban populations require, leaving them in a permanent state of catch-up.
- Even under these conditions, cities tend to offer substantially higher incomes and better access to services than the areas people are leaving, which is precisely what sustains the migration.
- The forces driving urban growth are the economic pull of cities over rural alternatives, the displacement effects of climate stress on agricultural land, and the concentration of infrastructure investment in urban centres.
- Some planned cities are motivated by existential necessity. Parts of Jakarta, currently the world's most populous city with nearly 42 million inhabitants, are sinking by as much as 25 centimetres per year, with sections at risk of being submerged by 2050, prompting Indonesia to develop Nusantara, a new political capital over 1,000 kilometres away.

## Manifestations

- Dhaka in Bangladesh is expected to become the world's largest city by mid-century with 52 million inhabitants, roughly the size of South Korea's total current population. Towards 2050, Africa's urban population is estimated to double from 700 million to 1.4 billion, almost the equivalent of the entire populations of the United States and the European Union moving into cities within less than 30 years.
- Megaregions are forming in Asia and Africa as neighbouring cities expand outwards until they merge into continuous urban corridors, such as India's emerging Delhi-Kolkata corridor along the Indo-Gangetic Plain and the Lagos-Abidjan corridor spanning five countries. Both exceed the capacity of governance infrastructures designed for individually separated cities. Megaregions can be products of megacities subsuming urban areas within their reach, or of several closely located cities morphing together. The number of megacities has quadrupled from eight in 1975 to 33 in 2025 and is projected to reach 37 by 2050.
- Rapidly expanding cities and megaregions will increase informal settlements globally. As new urban epicentres draw in workers, housing costs often exceed what is affordable to low-income migrants, who settle on the urban fringes. Informal settlements are not aberrations from planned urban development but its predictable offshoots, often characterised by self-organised construction along new highways or railways that provide access to the formal city economy. Informality moves into the periphery, the 'gray spaces', producing a tension between the formal core and the gray zones that formality itself generates at the margins.
- Urban populations in the rest of the world are also expanding, at a much slower pace. In Europe and North America, built-up areas expand faster than urban populations can occupy them, the opposite dynamic from fast-growing cities in Africa and Asia. Housing affordability is an issue across OECD countries, where close to half of low-income owners spent over 40% of disposable income on a mortgage in 2022, as did low-income renters.
- The flip side is the rapid depopulation of smaller cities and rural areas, risking their collapse. Around 3,000 cities experienced population decline between 2015 and 2025, most with fewer than 250,000 inhabitants, over a third in China and 17% in India, showing that urban growth and decline often occur simultaneously within the same country.
- In contrast to emergent informal milieus is the wave of master-planned satellite cities in Africa, South-East Asia and the Middle East. Over 90% of urban planning projects announced in the 21st century have or will take place in low-income economies. Egypt's New Administrative Capital, a 700 km2 development east of Cairo, is built to house government ministries and relieve pressure on a city of over 22 million. Tatu City near Nairobi is designed as a self-contained urban hub to attract investment, as is Nigeria's Enyimba Economic City. Saudi Arabia's The Line, now heavily scaled back, was meant to be a model concept city built for liveability, sustainability and urban innovation.

## Implications

- The world's new urban epicentres will not follow neat formulae of growth and development, nor necessarily be considered the pinnacle of urban achievement. Given the speed of their expansion, they will face circumstance-specific obstacles and completely novel forms of urban environment will emerge.
- The makeshift and the intermediary have traditionally been presented as developmental problems to solve, but cities that have had to improvise their way forward have developed a particular resourcefulness. Coupled with relatively youthful populations, these urban epicentres provide fertile ground for novel solutions to local and global problems. It is also in the margins where ideas and solutions to novel challenges might be sown.
- The future of urbanisation will be a set of deeply divergent urban worlds, shaped by the speed of each city's expansion, the capacity of its institutions, the climate vulnerability of its location, and the income level of the populations it absorbs. In some places, informal self-organisation will prove more adaptive than formal planning. In others, the gap between population growth and service delivery will become self-reinforcing, locking communities into persistent precarity. In others still, planned city experiments will attract capital and expertise while leaving the surrounding urban fabric largely untouched.
- What ties these trajectories together is the structural momentum behind them. Even where urban governance fails to keep pace, the cities will grow. The central question is what kinds of places this urbanisation produces and who bears the cost of the gaps it could leave behind.
