# Inversion of the population pyramid

## What it is

The global population is being fundamentally restructured through a shift from youth-heavy to increasingly senior-heavy societies, driven by sustained fertility decline and longer lifespans. This transition is universal in direction but uneven in pace. Some countries are already experiencing rapid ageing and population decline, while others still face continued population growth and younger age structures for decades to come. The result is a period where ageing, shrinking population and youth expansion coexist globally.

The knock-on consequences of this demographic inversion extend across societal domains. They strain pension, healthcare and education systems built for populations that were, on average, younger. They create imperatives for investment in AI and automation to offset a shrinking labour supply. They drive migration flows and affect intergenerational relations. And they shift the political calculus around conflict, innovation and institutional risk-taking.

## Sub-trends

- Uneven population growth concentration
- Sustained decline in fertility rate
- Rising life expectancy and ageing
- Increasing dependency burdens
- Migration pressures

## Uncertainties

- Will barriers to family formation, including housing costs, labour market insecurity, gender inequality and changing social norms, persist or intensify, further suppressing fertility rates?
- To what extent will international migration cushion the effects of ageing and workforce contraction in low-fertility countries?
- Is migration managed or disruptive, both at point of origin and destination?
- To what extent can productivity gains through technology and automation offset the economic effects of shrinking or ageing workforces?
- Will governments be able to adapt social systems such as pensions, healthcare and education to match changing population structures?
- Will lower-income countries be able to keep up with the needs of their rapidly growing young population?

## Drivers

- The latest century of human history has been marked by unprecedented population growth. For millennia, the global population was on a slow but steady increase, as high birth rates were largely offset by high death rates. Beginning in the mid-20th century, this rough equilibrium was broken. Although growth is now slowing, the world population is projected to rise to around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s (up from 8.2 billion in 2024). This future growth will occur mainly in Sub-Saharan Africa and Asia. More than one fifth of population growth between 2024 and 2054 is expected to come from just nine countries, including Angola, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Niger, Somalia and Tanzania, where populations are projected to at least double.
- At the core of this shift lies a sustained decline in fertility. Globally, fertility rates have fallen to around 2.3 children per woman, less than half the level observed in the 1950s. Even in many of the world's most populous countries, including India and China, fertility has dropped below the replacement level of approximately 2.1, making long-term population decline increasingly likely without sustained immigration.
- In many countries, people have fewer children than they desire, pointing to a gap between fertility intentions and outcomes driven by high childcare costs, job insecurity, difficulties in reconciling work and family life, and reproductive health. Fertility outcomes depend not only on financial support for families but also on gender dynamics. In France and Sweden, family policies combined with more equal care structures help sustain relatively higher fertility. In Japan and South Korea, where gender inequality and work-life imbalance persist, increased spending has had limited impact. In other countries, the preference to have fewer or no children is the deciding factor, as in Hungary, where baby loans and income tax exemptions for mothers among the most generous in the world have produced no durable rise in fertility.
- Alongside declining fertility, rising life expectancy is extending the upper end of the age distribution. Improvements in healthcare, nutrition and living standards have increased longevity across most regions, contributing to increasingly 'top-heavy' age structures. By 2060, 28% of the OECD population will be aged 65 or older, rising to roughly 37% in Italy and Japan.

## Manifestations

- More than one quarter of the global population already lives in countries where population growth has peaked, including China, Germany, Japan and the Russian Federation. Countries such as India, Indonesia, Nigeria, Pakistan and the US are projected to continue growing, with population peaks expected later in the century or beyond 2100. In the long term, even some of the most populous countries today will face a future where labour force expansion can no longer be taken for granted, where dependency ratios begin to rise, and where economic growth becomes increasingly reliant on productivity rather than demographic momentum.
- The inversion occurs across all regions but at an uneven pace. All societies face a demographic race against time. Some will be pressured to fundamentally restructure their economic and social welfare systems to account for rapidly ageing populations and shrinking workforces, while others will need to make sure that their expanding, youthful population is converted into a productive, healthy and utilised labour force.
- Ageing leads to financial constraints. Alzheimer's and other dementias alone will cost the world economy USD 14,513 billion between 2020 and 2050, putting pressure on pension and healthcare systems designed for a very different demographic outlook.
- As populations age, the growing political weight of older cohorts influences fiscal priorities, with increased support for pensions, healthcare and other forms of social protection. Ageing populations are associated with relatively lower public spending on education and lower long-run economic growth, reflecting a shift towards present-oriented welfare over future-oriented expenditure.
- Automation is being taken up in response. In Japan, faced with steep population decline, increased automation efforts can be seen in the context of the acute need to expand labour productivity and extend working lives. China is heavily investing in automation and AI to offset the negative economic impact of an ageing population.
- In youthful countries such as Nigeria, Tanzania and the Democratic Republic of Congo, the primary concerns relate to absorbing rapidly expanding labour forces into productive employment and ensuring sufficient investment in education, job creation, housing and infrastructure. Where this is achieved, it could generate a demographic dividend; where it is not, it could increase the risk of persistent unemployment, informality and social instability. This demographic momentum often coincides with mounting pressure on food systems and essential resources, even as fast-growing countries are expected to account for a growing share of global economic expansion.
- Migration is often seen as a solution to the twin challenges of growth and decline. The global stock of international migrants reached 304 million in 2024, nearly twice as many as in 1990. In several low-fertility destinations, including the United Arab Emirates, Germany, Italy, Portugal and the Czech Republic, immigration accounted for all population growth between 2000 and 2020. Advanced economies already experience societal polarisation and a rise in nativist politics partly in response to immigration. In countries with an outward flow, brain drain and loss of economic momentum become pressing. The African Development Bank estimates that 10 to 12 million young Africans enter the labour market each year while only around three million formal jobs are created; where this gap persists, a youth population that could have powered growth is pushed into unemployment and informality and pressured to seek opportunity abroad.

## Implications

- The growing imbalance will fundamentally alter the relationship between working populations and dependents.
- Ageing may lessen both the preferences and capacity for warfare in aged countries. A scarcity of young people tends to make 'wasting' their lives in war more costly, and their labour will be needed elsewhere in the economy, potentially pulling the international arena in the direction of a geriatric peace.
- Risk-averseness is a general characteristic of aged electorates, who tend to be more conservative than younger ones. Ageing populations will increasingly represent a governance dilemma, evident in the tension between systems that function as investments in the future (education, climate and the environment, infrastructure) and those oriented toward managing the present (pensions, sick care and eldercare). As retirees crowd out workers, the notion that each generation supports the previous one in old age will come under increased pressure.
- One could imagine a future society that is more peaceful, perhaps, but also more stagnant, less innovative, more nostalgic in its culture and politics, and much more demanding on the youth to perform their role as custodians of an ageing society rather than innovators. The generational tensions already evident where young people feel locked out of housing and asset markets could intensify in a future where the youth become simultaneously the most protected and the most extracted-from demographic.
- In most societies, informal eldercare is performed primarily by women, sometimes at the cost of labour market participation. An ageing society heightens the risk of a structural reinforcement of gender inequality, and may exacerbate declining fertility, since the unequal domestic burden on women is one of its documented drivers.
- Over the century, as more countries experience labour shortages, large-scale migration will be harder to resist politically, but the impetus to grow the labour force in receiving countries will need to be continuously balanced with the risk of political backlash.
- There will be an increasingly uneven distribution of labour, capital and institutional capacity. A growing share of global labour force expansion will be concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa, while capital, financial depth and institutional capacity remain concentrated in higher-income economies. This divergence is expected to reshape patterns of global interdependence and could generate new tensions around migration and investment flows.
- Two potential futures emerge. One in which intensifying competition for younger workers becomes the focal point of geopolitical bargaining. Another where automation and AI displace jobs at scale, so that labour scarcity is not as big an issue as expected, placing economic and social systems on new foundations and raising questions about who owns the output and how it is shared, and about the value of care work that markets still do not price.
- The great demographic inversion will not produce a single global outcome. Economies will face fundamentally different challenges, whether managing labour scarcity and fiscal pressure in ageing societies or creating employment and opportunity in younger ones. These asymmetries will shape economic performance, social organisation and political dynamics for decades to come.

## Figure: the East Asian demographic inversion

Two population pyramids side by side, each split into male and female halves, with five-year age bands from 0-4 at the bottom to 100+ at the top and a horizontal scale from 10% through 0% to 10%. Eastern Asia in 2000, with a population of just under 1.5 billion, is a broad-based triangle whose widest bands sit in the 25-39 brackets, tapering steadily upward with very narrow bands above 70. Eastern Asia in 2100, with a population of around 770 million, has inverted: the base below age 20 is narrow, the widest bands sit in the 70-89 brackets, and the profile bulges near the top before tapering at 100+. A sustained fertility decline narrows the base while longer lives fill out the top, and the region's population is expected to fall over the century to roughly half its size in 2000.
