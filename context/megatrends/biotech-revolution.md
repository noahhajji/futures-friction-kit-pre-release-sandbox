# The biotech revolution

## What it is

Biotechnology, the use of living organisms to make products and raw materials, is set to revolutionise the world over the next half-century. It enables us to transcend natural limitations, understand and manipulate genetic codes of plants and animals, create modified crops that are more resilient or require less fertiliser, develop new treatments for hereditary illnesses, and increase life expectancy, perhaps drastically. Over the course of this century, biotechnology will be integrated deeper into nearly all facets of modern society, including healthcare, food production, material design and energy production.

Every day when we open our fridge, we see the products of biotechnologies: bread made from selectively bred wheat, fruit juice with enzymes, or meat and dairy from animals raised on genetically modified feeds. Biotechnology is so central to the modern world that many of us would not be alive without it. High-yielding crop varieties increased yields by 44% between 1965 and 2010, reducing famine and helping sustain the rapidly growing populations of the late 20th century.

## Sub-trends

- Genomics becoming the operating system of life's essentials, medicine and food production
- Bioenergy leading to more sustainable heat and fuels
- Rise of biological carbon sequestration
- Emergence of bio-based materials
- Bio-enhanced food production
- AI-enhancement of biotech innovation

## Uncertainties

- Will ecological engineering be applied on a wide enough scale to counteract the effects of climate change?
- What regulatory frameworks will shape the rollout of emerging biotechnologies?
- Can cultivated (lab-grown) foods move from niche innovation to mainstream acceptance?
- How successfully will synthetic biology deliver on its promise of designing organisms fit for a changed world?
- What role will ethics play in an accelerated biotech revolution, if any?

## Drivers

- Food production continues to be one of biotechnology's most important frontiers, not only in total yield but increasingly in what ends up on a plate. Six billion people regularly consume milk or dairy products, and the land and water use demanded by conventional livestock, along with the emissions it produces, is a major driver of climate change and environmental degradation.
- Direct emissions from livestock production are a major source of greenhouse gases, making improvements in animal agriculture an important climate strategy.
- In shipping and air traffic, electrification is not a viable option, leaving a gap that advanced biofuels (fuels made from non-food biological material) are poised to fill. Past generations of biofuel production competed with food production for agricultural land; during the 2010-11 global food crisis, rising food prices, with land use for first-generation biofuels as a contributing factor, caused riots in Egypt that eventually contributed to the toppling of President Mubarak.
- The convergence of biotech with artificial intelligence may accelerate progress even further. AlphaFold, Google DeepMind's protein-structure prediction system, solved a problem in months that had occupied structural biologists for half a century, earning its creators the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2024. Drug discovery pipelines that once took vast infrastructure and decades are being compressed into years, computationally, by smaller teams at a fraction of the cost.
- Money is flowing into longevity research, with patrons like Amazon's Jeff Bezos and Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince turning their attention this way. Saudi Arabia's Hevolution Foundation has bankrolled USD 40 million of a USD 101 million XPrize competition for the first team to demonstrably reverse aspects of ageing by ten years.

## Manifestations

- Cultivated meat grabs the most headlines, but an arguably more significant prospect is precision fermentation in dairy production. CIFS research estimates that, based on the technology price curve, milk protein from precision fermentation might reach parity with milk from cows in 10-15 years and from then on start disrupting milk production.
- Research into the relationship between the rumen microbiome, feed composition and cattle genetics may help lower methane emissions significantly while maintaining productivity. Crops like wheat can be genetically modified to sequester more CO2 underground while increasing food productivity, though these techniques are at a very early stage. Grass-to-protein production could reduce the structural dependence on imported soy that ties American and Asian food systems to land-use decisions in South America; the feedstock is abundant, the land already in use, and the supply chain implications of even modest substitution are significant.
- Advanced contemporary biofuels largely avoid the land-use dilemma, using feedstocks that do not require agricultural land. The EU's RefuelEU Aviation Regulation (adopted in 2023) mandates 20% sustainable aviation fuel in 2035 and 70% in 2050, most of it from advanced biofuels.
- In January 2026, the US Food and Drug Administration approved a human clinical trial by Life Biosciences, co-founded by Harvard geneticist David Sinclair, testing a gene therapy targeting vision loss in patients with glaucoma and rare optic nerve conditions. The overarching ambition is the possibility of reversing ageing itself. Longevity research is often at odds with mainstream medical science, but the money flowing into the field makes it difficult to disregard.
- For the 200 million people worldwide dependent on insulin, manufactured using recombinant DNA techniques, the life-sustaining necessity of biotechnology is self-evident. Within a few years of the COVID-19 viral genome being published, mRNA vaccines were manufactured and administered to billions of people worldwide, perhaps the fastest and largest deployment of a biotechnology product in history. Christopher Voigt of MIT assesses that by 2030, most people in the developed world will have consumed, worn, used, or received medical treatment from products that involved synthetic biology somewhere in their production chain.
- Neglected diseases, including several tropical diseases affecting hundreds of millions of people, are a prime target for AI-powered drug discovery, which can accelerate the identification of drug candidates and cut the time and cost of bringing new treatments to market.

## Implications

- Longevity research is hugely contested, not just for scientific reasons but for the ethical questions it raises. The most significant is who gets access to treatment. If treatment to extend life significantly becomes mainstream or universally accessible, what happens to pension systems, housing markets and inheritance? Globally, its impacts on population growth and the Earth's already strained resources will have to be considered. Scepticism is warranted, but as with Artificial General Intelligence, considered science fiction two decades ago, longevity research may prove to be one of those rare fields where the science outpaces what human imagination thought possible.
- The use of biotechnology has been riddled with controversy, particularly surrounding GMO and human cloning. There is no single worldwide treaty that legally prohibits human reproductive cloning; the closest is the UN Declaration on Human Cloning (2005). A powerful taboo around cloning has nevertheless helped prevent an international biological arms race during a period when the technology remained unsafe and its benefits uncertain. The real test of that taboo will come if human enhancement becomes demonstrably safe and effective.
- The controversies will not disappear and will continue to shape the direction, speed of rollout and impact of biotech. Concerns and risks include ecosystem impacts, monopolisation issues (such as seeds preventing farmers from saving and replanting their own seed stock), and public trust issues, particularly regarding health. The very plausibility of the claim that COVID-19 was engineered in a laboratory and escaped, whatever its truth, shows the potentially very negative consequences that biotechnology could have.
- Despite risks and controversies, biotech will enable the redesign of biological systems for better health, more resistant food, sustainable fuels and new materials. The challenges of this century will not be solved by any single discipline, but it is hard to construct a credible path through them that does not, at some point, run through biotechnology.
