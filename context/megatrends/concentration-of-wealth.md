# Concentration of wealth

## What it is

The gains of economic growth have never been distributed evenly across the globe, not between countries, not within them, and not between those who own capital and those who sell labour. But the present configuration of global wealth accumulation constitutes something historically distinct, not least in its extremity. As the world gets progressively richer, more productive and more populous, the benefits of this sustained growth are far from shared equally. Growing wealth inequality presents future challenges to state capacity, the ability to meet existential challenges, and perhaps even to the future of democracy itself.

## Sub-trends

- The 'oligarchising' of politics
- Ownership of platforms, democracy's news and information 'infrastructure', in the hands of the super-wealthy
- Accounting and tax rules lagging new models of wealth and value creation
- Declining labour share of income
- Intergenerational wealth transfer and the concentration of inheritance
- Asset-based stratification

## Uncertainties

- Can governments act on wealth redistribution before inherited wealth concentrations become too entrenched to reverse?
- Can democracies effectively counteract 'oligarchising' when wealth easily evades national jurisdictions?
- How will wealth concentration among platform owners evolve under potential regulatory pressure?
- Can global collaboration on taxation and financial market controls become a stronger counterweight to inequality?
- Can redistributive instruments such as a universal basic income compensate for a falling labour share of income, and can a national tax base in the exposed country fund them when the productivity gains accrue elsewhere, i.e. Chennai vs. California?

## Drivers

- Some causes of this extreme divergence have been decades in the making: the deregulation of capital markets, capital accumulation outpacing economic growth, and the gradual weakening of workers' bargaining power relative to capital across much of the world.
- Other structural drivers are newer. Platform monopolies generate returns at near-zero marginal cost, enabling rapid wealth accumulation. The list of top wealth holders today has an overrepresentation of technology platform owners whose fortunes were built on this mechanism.
- Platform-based business models have opened a gap between where economic value is created and where it can be measured, taxed and redistributed. Through the 2010s, some of the world's most valuable companies generated extraordinary returns with comparatively little physical capital, their value sitting in intangible assets, networks and globally scalable digital infrastructure. That may now be changing, as the same firms spent several hundred billion dollars in 2026 on data centres, chips and power, tying their value back to places, grids and permits.
- Accounting, financial and especially taxation systems remain poorly adapted to both versions of the model. The OECD mechanism for shifting taxing rights to the countries where users are, Amount A of Pillar One, has still not come into force, and more than twenty national digital services taxes have filled the gap. Much of the wealth of the very rich is held in appreciating shares, which generally generate tax revenues only when sold, giving owners little incentive to realise gains.
- Recent international tax negotiations suggest the principal barriers are political rather than technical: many of the necessary mechanisms exist, but access to them is shaped by the relative bargaining power of states and corporations. The clearest example is the January 2026 'side by side' agreement, which largely exempts US multinational companies from the OECD's global minimum tax rules.
- A new and perhaps more durable dynamic is the transmission of accumulated wealth between generations on a scale unprecedented in recent history. Market-generated concentration is, in principle, reversible through labour market dynamics, taxation or shifts in competitive conditions. Generational wealth does not depend on the continued performance of the inheritor and is protected by trusts, foundations or offshore vehicles designed to minimise erosion through taxation or market risk. The generations set to inherit the so-called Great Wealth Transfer, primarily Generation X and Millennials, are the first to do so after four decades of intensified accumulation at the top.
- AI is a 'capital-biased' innovation whose returns flow disproportionately to capital rather than labour. This winner-takes-most dynamic echoes past industrial revolutions, from textile mills to industrial robots, which concentrated ownership of capital-intensive machinery while lowering skill requirements and creating an oversupply of replaceable workers.

## Manifestations

- Between 2000 and 2024, the richest 1% of the world population captured 41% of all new wealth, while the bottom half received just 1%. The top 10% own three-quarters of global wealth, while the bottom half holds only 2%. The top 1% alone, roughly the adult population of the United Kingdom, controls 37% of global wealth. Fewer than 6,000 individuals hold 3% of all wealth, more than the entire bottom half of the world's adult population.
- Corporate tax revenues have broadly held up, so the central dispute is not only how much tax is collected but which country has the right to collect it. The taxation of concentrated corporate and platform wealth is becoming less a question of institutional design than a contest over who has sufficient leverage to determine, and benefit from, the rules.
- The conversion of concentrated wealth into political influence, the 'oligarchising' of politics, ranges from buying political support to consolidating ownership of key societal institutions and infrastructure. A study covering 120 countries over 23 years found a negative effect of economic concentration on a nation's level of democracy, with corporate political activity as a likely mechanism. Pressures include the funding of foundations, think tanks and policy-planning networks; campaign finance; the shaping of public opinion through media and expert commentary; and constitutional provisions that protect property and create veto points at which change can be blocked.
- Increasing wealth inequality has been associated with a reduction in the proportion of individuals identifying with the subjective middle class, the group most supportive of democratic governance.
- 2026 marked the first year when social media and video networks were the most widely used source of news globally (54% of all audiences). Legacy media must lean on corporate scaling or partner with massive tech ecosystems to maintain reach. Social media networks and algorithmic search engines, voting-controlled in several cases by a single individual, act as primary news and information gatekeepers worldwide. Algorithmic ranking by predicted engagement creates new risks, accentuated by the fact that dissemination precedes fact-checking.
- The rise of 'mega-philanthropy', itself a product of wealth concentration, shows that where public capacity recedes (or was never present), what replaces it is defined by those who own the capital. That replacement is largely unaccountable, and its priorities follow donor preference rather than public consensus.

## Implications

- Wealth polarisation does not in itself produce risks to state capacity, democratic stability and governance. But the uneven playing field it creates contributes to hollowing out the democratic process and makes policies and outcomes that systematically favour the interests of the wealthy more likely.
- The qualitative shift in the social logic of wealth towards inheritance may further widen the gap between economic position and social contribution, something close to the emergence of a new aristocracy.
- When the greatest fortunes sat in oil, retail or property, the asset and the political voice it afforded were separable. When the asset is society's information, news and communications infrastructure, the asset and the megaphone are the same object.
- As AI graduates from tool to embedded infrastructure, one potential outcome, even with productivity growth, is that absent political intervention it contributes to widening inequalities. Past industrial revolutions were followed by counterbalances: organised labour movements, government regulations, the expansion of the welfare state. What the redistributive institutions of the future will look like is an open question. Universal basic income is one proposition, yet it is unclear how it would be financed: the productivity gains of platform and AI infrastructure accrue to owners concentrated in a small number of jurisdictions, while the labour markets those technologies reshape are global. A parliament in Delhi cannot legislate a tax rate in California. Proposals for a coordinated minimum tax on the very wealthy, and the ongoing negotiation of a UN framework convention on international tax cooperation, are attempts to close exactly this gap.
- For non-inheriting majorities, wealth-based stratification means labour market participation may be increasingly insufficient to generate the asset accumulation necessary for retirement security and housing access. Capital returns concentrate in metropolitan areas where asset prices are highest, producing 'geographies of discontent' and a risk of political backlash.
- Wealth concentration drives other risks through the social polarisation it foments. Democratic systems eroding through elite capture face another angle of attack from the political mobilisation of those who believe they are not seeing their fair share of growth. The rise of populism in the 2010s and 2020s is also a story of the inequalities produced by a globalised and stratified economy. Some of the most significant upheavals in modern history, pre-revolutionary France, tsarist Russia, Weimar Germany, were preceded by periods of intense inequality.
- The risks are heightened by the lock-in of structural constraints that make course-correction increasingly difficult. Where governments depend on private capital to finance public functions, through debt, de-risking and public-private partnerships, the range of democratic choice narrows, constrained by the preferences of the private wealth on which fiscal states depend. Rebuilding redistributive institutions requires coalitions that wealth concentration makes harder to assemble. This could create legitimacy problems for a social contract resting on the premise that effort, skill and participation generate a proportional meritocratic reward.
- The unprecedented concentration of wealth has not been matched by any comparable growth in the capacity to solve shared problems, whether stemming climate change or eradicating hunger. The question for the coming decades is whether fiscal, regulatory and democratic capacity can be rebuilt at the scale at which capital now operates. How it is answered will determine whether the concentration of wealth remains an economic outcome or hardens into a structural feature of how societies are governed.
