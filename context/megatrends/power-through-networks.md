# Power through networks

## What it is

Power Through Networks describes the long-term shift from territorial, state-centred sovereignty and power towards authority that is distributed, networked and overlapping. Critical social, economic and security functions increasingly depend on transnational digital infrastructures that no single state controls. Authority is redistributed rather than drained away, since the same networks that put a function beyond one state's reach can extend another's.

Meta, Alphabet, Amazon and other infrastructural giants defy easy categorisation as mere market participants. They are companies, but also private entities grown into para-states with unprecedented political and economic power and geographical reach, achieved through ownership of the infrastructure on which the daily functioning of society, global commerce and states increasingly depend. They exercise power in their own interests, sometimes to the point of influencing elections. But the reverse holds as well: states govern through these private networks, treating their reach as an extension of national jurisdiction and their services as levers that can be tightened or withdrawn. The general picture is a dynamic and fluid reconfiguration, of 'new territories superimposed on older territories'.

## Sub-trends

- Hyperscaler dominance over critical digital infrastructure
- Divergence of competing infrastructure models: US commercial, EU regulatory, Chinese state-aligned
- Shift from 'territorial' to 'operational' sovereignty (from jurisdiction over territory to control over infrastructure)
- Increasing state and public sector dependency on private networks
- Growing political influence of US-based and Chinese tech sectors, domestically and internationally
- States projecting power through privately operated infrastructure

## Uncertainties

- Will most developing economies come to depend on US or on Chinese digital infrastructure and solutions?
- Which public institutions will struggle to discharge their mandates when the infrastructure they depend on sits outside their jurisdiction?
- Will service suspension become a routine instrument of statecraft, or remain exceptional enough to be shocking?
- To what extent will Europe succeed in its goal of achieving digital sovereignty through homegrown digital infrastructure?
- Will the gap between governing role and governing seat ever close formally?

## Drivers

- The megatrend has its origins in the transition of the internet from a government-sponsored network to a commercial one in the mid-1990s. Its historical roots go deeper: Britain's 'All-red Line' telegraph system safeguarded imperial communications though it was mostly privately owned and operated, a past version of today's sovereign cloud.
- Ownership of digital infrastructure is increasingly concentrated, and in most of the world that concentration sits with a handful of US-based hyperscalers. As of 2025, Amazon, Microsoft and Alphabet held 63% of the worldwide cloud market.
- Undersea cables, the arteries of the digital world, were historically built by regulated, route-neutral, multi-stakeholder carrier consortiums. Digital giants are now vertically integrating this infrastructure with their services. In 2010, Google, Facebook, Microsoft and Amazon had invested in just one long-distance undersea cable between them; by the end of 2024, they had stakes in more than 60 of about 600 worldwide. Content providers' share of total international cable capacity was 6.3% in 2010 and is expected to reach 78% by 2027.
- Data centres, fibre optic lines, cloud servers and cellular towers add to these material foundations, which suggest long-term durability.
- Nations now exercise power alongside and sometimes through 'virtual' or 'supplemental' sovereigns, tech companies and platforms exercising commercial development of and control over critical software and hardware.

## Manifestations

- Governments issue currency and maintain monetary sovereignty, but everyday payments and transactions are increasingly mediated by private transactional networks. Privately owned infrastructure, undersea cables, cloud computing, satellite networks and data centres, has become as crucial as public roads, grids and postal services, or more so.
- In the United Kingdom, 95% of central and local public sector organisations spent budget on hyperscale cloud services in fiscal year 2023/24, mostly with US-based providers.
- As passports and driving licences are supplemented by digital IDs, biometrics and verification via social media, citizens become 'users' as well as national subjects. Individual health data is simultaneously personal information safeguarded by national law and a valuable resource for private actors in healthcare and insurance.
- In politics, maintaining a presence in the 'new public square', digital platforms hosted by private firms, is a necessity for reaching constituents and voters. Political critique of the platforms must often take place on the platforms themselves. Governance relies heavily on private software and utilities, so even functions only the state is authorised to perform are executed on systems it does not own.
- Not everything is being uprooted. Land and property, many welfare functions, and criminal justice remain more classically territorial.
- In August 2026, a US presidential memorandum authorised vetted private cybersecurity firms to carry out offensive cyber operations against international criminal networks targeting Americans, breaking from decades-old policy restricting private entities to network defence. Here the state extended its own reach through private actors rather than ceding ground to them.
- During Covid-19, emergency pandemic response, a core function of territorial sovereignty, hinged on smartphone operating systems, cloud services and API standards outside state control.
- After Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Ukraine appealed to ICANN, the US-based non-profit that coordinates the Domain Name System, to exclude Russia from the network. ICANN opted for neutrality, but the refusal was itself an exercise of authority. Starlink, a privately owned civilian network, became essential to Ukraine's ability to defend itself, used particularly for drones and artillery targeting; as of 2026 Starlink operates roughly 59% of all active satellites. The reliance made Ukraine's military more effective, but that effectiveness could be revoked by decisions taken elsewhere.
- In the EU, a sustained institutional response has taken the form of a digital sovereignty agenda and efforts to reduce dependency on non-EU infrastructure through regulation and targeted investments.
- In China, tech giants such as Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance and Huawei have built the country's critical platform and network layers as formally private companies. Rather than replacing them, the state has attached itself to them through 'special management shares' with privileged voting rights, party cells within companies, and at times overt pressure or forced rectification of business leaders, most famously Alibaba founder Jack Ma. Beyond its borders, China's Digital Silk Road has financed fibre-optic cable, mobile networks, satellites and cloud capacity across Africa, Southeast Asia and Central Asia, on the terms of the Chinese state and tech providers. The difference from the American arrangement is one of directness rather than of kind.

## Implications

- The question of who governs, operates and holds authority over the systems that facilitate the functioning of societies and states has become a defining question for the 21st century.
- Infrastructure owners are, in effect, in governing roles but without formal governing seats. As the logic of the network supersedes the logic of the map, the borders of authority and agency are becoming vague and overlapping.
- Power, agency and sovereignty are not zero-sum. For states reliant on private networks, augmentation can coincide with vulnerable dependence. Digital infrastructure owners also pose direct challenges to territorial sovereignty; both things are true at the same time.
- Even the most ambitious European programme will only reshape the terms of dependency, perhaps resulting in a more even balance between homegrown and US-based providers, rather than eliminating it. A fundamental change in ownership patterns from private to fully public would require a momentous historical shake-up, such as a forced breakup or nationalisation of digital infrastructure. Today's regulatory backlash should be understood as a negotiation of the terms of dependency rather than a potential disruption of the trend.
- The next phase may be determined by the global struggle over whose infrastructure model becomes the default, and where, as the private infrastructure on which sovereignty depends may increasingly be Chinese rather than American in origin in significant portions of the world. The rerouting of sovereignty may be replicated under a different flag but remain a defining feature of the 21st century.
