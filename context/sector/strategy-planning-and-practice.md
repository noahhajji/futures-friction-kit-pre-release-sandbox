# Strategy, planning and practice

## Practices

- Practitioners cite external references such as sprint methods, facilitation cookbooks, strategy textbooks and business-model canvases as their go-to toolbox. (12 mentions)
- Scenario planning is valued as robustness training rather than prediction, asking what makes sense regardless of which future unfolds; organisations run scenario days with large leadership groups, though some exercises are experienced as too fast and short. (2 mentions)
- Fast-moving organisations plan in short horizons because detailed long-range plans lose relevance: roadmaps of one to two years, quarterly framing for returns, a 'long-term' horizon of about five years, and a stated need to still orient toward longer-term structural change.
- Process knowledge built up by a single internal process lead has lasting value and could be democratised as a best-practice account of good choices and pitfalls; running strategy work internally rather than with external consultants keeps that knowledge in the organisation.
- Staff who took part in a structured strategy process valued clear structure and governance, a shared goal, a single point of coordination and one person holding a strategically informed overview of where the organisation was heading.
- Mission statements along digital-first, agenda-setting lines precede strategic focus areas; analogue templates are digitised with AI tools whose quality the user cannot judge.
- Structured strategy processes in use: shared situation analysis and environmental scan, scenarios for degree of digital development, dilemma sliders stacked across units to reveal hidden disagreement, non-linear priority voting, explicit involvement plans by organisational level, phased timelines, and planning in detail two weeks out, overview three months out, direction two years out.
- Process-management tools in use are experienced by many as old-school project management used mainly for time registration, which stalls the incentive to use them, while a minority value them for long-running projects because decisions can be traced by newcomers.
- A simple two-axis effort-versus-value or impact framework, worked in silence with sticky notes and dot-voting rather than open brainstorming, is used to place and prioritise ideas at a realistic size.
- Some organisations work hypothesis-driven at experiment scale, storing hypotheses in a documentation tool, but such tools are static and hard to retrieve from, prompting experiments with a conversational AI layer over existing documentation.

## Constraints

- Culture and implementation are where organisations struggle most: they are good at workshops and ideas, but no one owns implementation, no dedicated resources exist, and the idea dies once routine takes over; the difference between organisations that succeed at long-term innovation and those stuck in optimisation is execution. (4 mentions)
- Shift rosters set roughly two months ahead under collective agreements mean any change affecting staff must be planned that far in advance, so implementation requires forward planning combined with continuous adjustment and significant investment in involvement and coordination.
- Not everyone in a leadership group can work at the same level of abstraction, so strategic work is driven by fewer people; frontline staff cannot reasonably be asked to imagine away their own jobs, so the boldest strategic thinking rarely comes from them.
- There can be a gap of years between change in the wider world and a media organisation's ability to implement it; switching a whole production flow to a new format can take a year and a half.
- An AI strategy tends to become outdated before it even takes effect.
- The three-horizons vocabulary is not self-evident to the people expected to use it. It is described as a muscle still in training, which has to be made simple and tied to everyday work and existing strategies before it helps, and a tool should not assume that its user knows the framework or the literature behind it.

## Needs

- Organisations want a shared vocabulary and a way to document what has already worked, so that ongoing transformation can be formalised, followed up with goals and shared learning, and used to onboard new staff. (3 mentions)
- Long-term strategic direction must be distinguished from short-term flexible execution; an innovation tool should support long horizons without pretending everything can be planned in detail, and the open question is how to make technology and staff mindset ready for a day that has not yet arrived.
- Organisations need methods to separate noise from real shifts: whether a signal is strong enough, how large an investment is appropriate, which competencies to build now, and when to scale or stop an experiment.
- An AI-mediated trend radar is proposed as a use case for an analytics workflow.

## Beliefs

- Good implementation makes change faster and poor process and culture understanding makes it slower; leadership can decide direction and bring in technology relatively easily, whereas moving the actual people is the hard part, and iterative change only reaches a radical goal if the right process understanding and culture already exist.
- Strategy originates at the top but cannot be implemented without ownership and translation at tactical and operational levels; the levels work with different knowledge, decisions and time horizons, and AI work in media happens mostly at the operational content level rather than at the tactical or strategic knowledge level, so a liaison between levels is essential.
- Succeeding in closed decision phases depends on understanding the specific people in the room rather than on frameworks; the process lead's job is to hear patterns in what leaders say and operationalise them, since phases are similar everywhere but their value depends on facilitation and adaptation.
- Organisational change is framed as managed decline of some parts, repurposing of others and room for new things to emerge, or as an existing and an emerging culture growing side by side; the organisation readies itself for a future it cannot force but knows is coming.
- Uncertainty is paralysing when treated as a problem to solve before acting and productive when treated as a design parameter; fast decisions on partial information are possible where staff can be trusted.
- A foresight output such as a trend report can have limited direct application yet still create value through the discussion and process it triggers.
- Imagining what news delivery could look like five years out and challenging why a fixed news format should be taken for granted is valued as a move, even though such questions are rarely taken seriously in the industry.
- The main barrier to taking future-driven innovation seriously is a lack of courage to accept that it cannot be fully described or reduced to a formula; the interrogatory mindset of investigative journalism maps well onto futures thinking.
- The direction a media organisation says it works towards is often read differently by top management, by staff and by the outside world: the top finds it clear where the others do not. Innovation then scatters, with nothing to hold a result up against and no measure of success, and whether a clear core exists shows up again and again in which innovations succeed.
