# Innovation process

## Practices

- Most processes start from unexamined assumptions that sit as a shadow over innovation work; a few practitioners use assumption testing individually rather than as a shared method, and organisations want a tool that surfaces, structures and visualises the assumptions in project material so a team can test them systematically. (7 mentions)
- New ideas typically originate with a journalist, from a problem, or from inspiration seen at another organisation, online or at a conference; they arise from several siloed directions rather than one shared structure, get discussed informally until they gain verbal traction, and are shelved quickly if the responsible manager lacks capacity. (6 mentions)
- Live conversational-listening tools that mirror themes, tensions and decisions from a discussion are being prototyped and expected in mainstream collaboration platforms; AI has been reported to outperform humans at spotting outliers across large interview sets. (3 mentions)
- Some development processes are heavily iterative, with regular review of what works, and look to the software world and agile approaches for method. (2 mentions)
- Some organisations run a structured involvement cadence, with recurring idea-and-investment meetings and periodic all-staff development sessions, and a looping process from need through idea, involvement, decision, execution and evaluation feeding back into a shared record of what has worked.
- Practitioners run unsanctioned 'guerrilla' projects, building a quick MVP first and pitching leadership only once the use case is compelling, because formal processes would have meant the projects never happened; this becomes harder as processes formalise, and greater transparency of decision-making would make it impossible.
- Some staff hide their AI use from colleagues out of embarrassment, and unstructured output from humans predates AI.
- Reviewing a product a year after launch surfaces material for reconsidering the approach; practitioners argue such discovery should be continuous on everything the organisation builds, so it stays focused on committed priorities instead of many small uncoordinated initiatives.
- Small technical projects that worked from day one struggle to get follow-through for lack of time and anyone available to roll them out; promising automations go unadopted pending migrations or because outcome quality is uncertain.
- AI assistants are already routinely part of individuals' idea-generation, with many recent solution ideas developed in dialogue with an assistant rather than alone.
- Where work can be reused for a new platform, the path from idea to decision moves quickly, though this is not true of most innovation work.
- Early experiments rarely produce a finished product but build competencies, readiness, networks and a shared language, and the ability to react quickly when a shift accelerates.

## Constraints

- Good ideas often go nowhere because there is no clear route for who to bring them to; people feel alone with their ideas, ideas die inside large internal programmes before reaching senior leadership, and internal advocacy for change gets encouraging feedback and then silence for months. (5 mentions)
- Ambitions to work iteratively turn into a waterfall model roughly nine times out of ten, because waterfall is ingrained, easy to relate to and forced by time and resource pressure; there is little room to iterate and test, discovery is unstructured, there is no consistent way of working, and temporary fixes become permanent.
- In a typical editorial-product workflow an idea is discussed with the manager, involves many staff in workshops, proceeds on the promise of savings, and either strands for lack of prioritisation or survives only because a specific desk carries it; choosing among many possible paths and arriving at a deployable product are challenges in themselves.
- Organisations do not talk to their users directly enough and are reactive about innovation, acting only once a problem has surfaced; they assume what users want or ask in ways that confirm decisions already made, even though behavioural data is more honest than focus groups.
- Proposals discussed with editorial teams and managers often receive no clear, unambiguous yes; vague responses from leadership create a recurring pattern of unclear buy-in.

## Needs

- Prioritised requirements for an innovation sparring tool: frictionless, simple, valuable on first use, secure, cheap, platform-agnostic, restrictable to a small group, able to challenge assumptions and give concrete future-oriented recommendations, able to work across horizons, hackable, role-adaptive, source-transparent, visual where assumptions and management communication are concerned, and able to play a provocative devil's advocate that imposes constraints. (7 mentions)
- Organisations cannot do everything themselves and would value outside help connecting the dots between initiatives, surfacing practices and ideas from outside the media industry, and simulating how audiences or advertisers might respond to a proposed idea. (3 mentions)
- A check of the assumptions behind an idea belongs at the very start of product development, as a required step among the other checks an idea must pass, before anything is written down. Few people in a news organisation have the time to think a plan through, and leaving the check to each product manager's choice is not expected to work. (3 mentions)
- Experiments need portfolio management: an explicit mechanism to see which experiments exist, which horizon they belong to, what resources and metrics apply, which to stop or scale, and how to move them between horizons, since long-horizon work does not land in the next horizon automatically and areas such as AI move faster than expected.
- To keep innovation projects alive as they near their goal, practitioners propose status updates, reminders, agent support for building a pitch, quick demonstrations on the organisation's own stack, sparring for good process and help qualifying decisions and convincing leadership.
- Open questions at each stage of a proposed innovation process: vision and anchoring at the need stage, language at the idea stage, how many to involve and how to present work, and resourcing, return and KPIs at the decision stage.
- Practitioners want a tool that intervenes partway through an innovation process, before resources are spent, to redirect processes heading for the wrong result, and want to map how their process actually works today and where its pain points sit.
- Analysts want to automate personalised insight delivery to editorial desks and surface patterns they would not see in raw numbers, and internal chatbots over policy documents with human escalation are proposed.
- Practitioners want ready-made building blocks that already work rather than building from scratch, noting that a large share of what counts as innovation never reaches production.

## Beliefs

- Editorial is often the biggest bottleneck for change: organisations with AI-ready data foundations did not act for lack of leadership interest, and editors of the old school block changes to established formats on the assumption that readers want what they are used to and that the organisation knows best.
- Some staff frame the desired outcome as an internal community where ideas spark off one another rather than a revenue tool, while questioning whether people are willing to share ideas at all, which they frame as a question of ownership over ideas.
- Value-driven organisations with a clear mission and journalists retaining unique human expertise are believed to have a viable future; an AI cannot replace a specialist's twenty years of accumulated knowledge though it can support parts of the task.
- Innovation processes are changing because a working prototype can now be built fast enough to bring a test group to leadership within weeks, whereas many good ideas historically stalled on not knowing how a proposal reaches leadership.
- A tool that only draws on one team's history risks becoming an echo chamber that keeps re-solving the same systemic problems; insight into other teams' problems is what would let a team change direction.
- Whenever the organisation commits to a new bet it should set clear objectives and articulate what it expects; once goals are set, an AI agent nudging the organisation toward that shared direction is welcome.
- Journalists prefer not to write routine data-driven items that could be generated from good data, preferring to go and talk to the people involved.
- It has to be cheap to fail. A quick prototype in people's hands gets a response at once and says far more about the real need, what can be done and where the potential lies, and its failures hold that information. In older IT projects a failure was a catastrophe, because half a year and millions had already gone into it.
