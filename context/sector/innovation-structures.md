# Innovation structures

## Practices

- Organisations that succeed at long-term innovation rely on cross-functional teams on clearly defined problems, embed innovation in daily operations, give teams a mandate to test quickly, protect recurring cross-disciplinary time, run incremental work in sprints, and structure themselves around experiments rather than production. (11 mentions)
- Several organisations have set up small cross-departmental AI teams working across desks, with editorial perspectives included as needed, self-organising around existing roles with protected time for the work, and given either a cost-saving goal or a deliberately ambitious one. (10 mentions)
- Recurring, open, informal AI dialogue forums without agenda or minutes can grow quickly and be copied across departments; building cultural 'frequency' around AI through such formats is where some organisations invest most energy. (8 mentions)
- In current data and automation workflows an external request form is ranked by project leaders, developers are brought in late so information is lost, and communication runs through intermediaries; participants sketched a future state where the developer goes out proactively to understand problems and connects requests to existing ideas.
- Spin-off companies from internal innovation can grow to serve external markets, at which point they grow away from the parent's needs and take the people who built them, making the pattern hard to sustain.
- Peer organisations across the Nordics have historically struggled to collaborate because priorities rarely align, though the AI shift is prompting more contact; bringing in high-profile external experts to present scenarios to large leadership groups is effective at paving the way for perspectives insiders struggled to convey.
- Some organisations use no formal innovation process and consider their lived daily culture their primary innovation tool; they have no innovation group and do not label their work innovation, which makes a pluggable innovation toolkit hard to picture.
- Adjacent fields show a contextual assistant can open doors to a process, support drafting and translate complex arguments for decision-makers, not only give advice.
- Protected morning focus blocks with no calls or email, and deliberate breaks, are used to create room for thinking; structured reflection is an organisational capacity many organisations lack.
- Where no structure around innovation existed, establishing one within a few years meant ideas were carried forward rather than lost; innovation leads offer themselves as a contact point and propose communities for dialogue about innovation.

## Constraints

- Large research-based innovation partnerships struggle: broad partner bases and heavy formal change processes are innovation-killing, and framing everything around responsibility sits in tension with fast continuous improvement.

## Needs

- Where innovation work should be formally anchored within a media organisation is an unresolved question with no shared internal view; the most siloed organisations, where different editorial units think in completely different ways, would need a significant push to adopt a shared framework. (4 mentions)
- A good local prototype is not sufficient; organisations need an internal ecosystem in which solutions can be implemented and scaled across the organisation.

## Beliefs

- 'Innovation theatre', labs, reports and experiments that display activity without producing change, is a recognised risk, as is a tool nobody uses or a framework that ends up as a PDF filed away; a product does not by itself create a long-term mindset. (5 mentions)
- A lab inside the organisation is seen as unable to drive transformative change because existing systems are too slow; a separate AI-native unit is argued to get a clearer mandate, resources and the chance to design processes from scratch.
- Hackathons and ideation weeks generate enthusiasm and concrete ideas across departments, but follow-through is weak: ideas rarely reach production, editorial representation is thin, and it is unclear what to pause and what to prioritise afterwards.
- Innovation success ultimately depends on the people carrying it forward; it produces changes in thinking more than direct revenue, emerges in clusters, and in uncertain times organisations should rely on iteration and hypothesis-driven learning rather than fixed structures.
- Experimental units have been closed in reorganisations after failing to produce enough or to communicate their value; the lab model lost support in favour of experimenting inside products, which pushed innovation toward incremental work, though early experimentation left a higher AI maturity.
- Bringing in talented people is not enough without a pipeline that turns ideas into visible outcomes, or recruits leave as fast as they arrived; restricting a new tool to a small chosen group makes adoption far easier than an organisation-wide rollout.
- What happens in practice often differs from stated ambitions, and organisations constrained by the pace they feel they must keep keep doing things the way they are used to; outside inspiration is welcomed as a way out of that pattern.
- Change is most effective when it comes from the organisation's periphery rather than its core.
