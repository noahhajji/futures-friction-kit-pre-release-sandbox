# Sector conditions

## Practices

- Automation can open new business models and B2B relationships while raising ethical challenges of its own; a problem-first approach challenges every technology request with what problem it solves and whether it is the right one, parking many.
- Some organisations have reorganised their digital side as a product organisation and moved technology from taking orders to sharing ownership of spending decisions, while professionalisation has lengthened the path from idea to test.
- Legacy channels still generate significant revenue and will run for years, so organisations discuss delivering the same content through new technology while making digital products more dynamic.
- Format decisions driven by what users actually did, rather than what they said or the team's gut feeling, can succeed when an organisation briefly breaks its consensus-seeking pattern through logic and psychological safety.

## Constraints

- Many publishers work with decades-old systems and lack technical capacity or an innovation mindset; supporting them matters because where journalism disappears misinformation fills the space, giving sustainable journalism a wider democratic role. (6 mentions)
- The main obstacle to restructuring around AI-native or agent-fed workflows is that a viable business model for that future does not yet exist; an organisation rewiring its newsroom to feed AI agents today would risk closing quickly.
- Growth mechanisms that relied on readers sharing journalism on social media have stopped working as platform dynamics shifted, and some organisations have left platforms earlier than their audiences.

## Beliefs

- AI adoption varies sharply by geography and maturity: some markets are far ahead and Europe is seen as more cautious; some organisations have a dedicated AI implementation lead while others have no clear owner and a 'wild west' of self-chosen tools. (9 mentions)
- Widespread layoffs raise the question of whether media organisations have the right people for a transformed future; defensive moats are scarce, and most organisations extrapolate the present rather than holding a clear three-to-five-year vision of what competitive space to occupy. (3 mentions)
- Vendors and organisations expect to learn from markets and users with a more mature relationship to AI, and see international contexts as operating in a different reality from the Danish one. (3 mentions)
- Vendors position sovereignty, trust, quality and control over data as differentiators as core AI capabilities such as transcription become commodities; 'sovereign' must specify where data, models and control actually sit.
- Initiatives should not be built for yesterday's market; organisations locked into legacy systems and outdated market understanding risk optimising a model losing relevance, and fragmented information sources put the relevance of established media itself in question.
- Newsrooms need to move from production-heavy workflows to treating news as knowledge and intelligence, and every media organisation increasingly has to become a technology company; refusing to build the infrastructure of the AI era risks losing control of distribution as happened with the printing press.
- What counts as innovative is not the activity itself but the scale a technology makes viable as a business model; the real innovation is often the enabling infrastructure rather than the visible product.
- Today's digital news structure is 'paper on a screen'; more fluid content forms are expected to change that structure totally, but such thinking is barely discussed in industry standards bodies.
- Innovation can be about finding the next revenue-generating product or business model for when the existing business weakens.
- The question every newsroom has to keep returning to is what the core value of information is today and which jobs it does for its audience; nobody has an answer to it.
