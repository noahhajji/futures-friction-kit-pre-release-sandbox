# Culture and psychology

## Practices

- Startup-style principles, acting on an idea at once and saying no wherever possible, shape some organisations' early culture.

## Constraints

- A cultural commitment to journalism as it is, and the trust that news depends on, make radical experiments with format feel like a break in identity. (4 mentions)
- Some employees actively resist AI because they fear it will take their job; this fear is psychological rather than technical, confronts people with questions about their security, and cannot simply be talked away. (3 mentions)
- Many teams keep running a familiar, comfortable routine even when it is not the best long-term approach. (2 mentions)
- Where staff turnover is low and hiring and firing are constrained, culture must evolve the people in place, which is slow and needs sustained resourcing; organisations must also keep serving user groups still on existing formats.
- Highly compliant, rules-driven cultures produce continuous improvement but almost no real innovation, ideas failing on 'not invented here' once they must scale; with nearly all staff oriented to routine operations, expecting otherwise without changing that reality is pointless.

## Beliefs

- Danish media innovation culture is seen as less diverse and more conservative than elsewhere in the Nordics: recruitment draws on known networks and legacy structures, media professionals are rarely trained in structured methods, and organisations with a high average age struggle to understand younger users. (6 mentions)
- Psychological safety is the foundation for experimenting with undefined futures: people need to feel safe raising half-formed ideas and asking naive questions, otherwise they simulate experiments with predefined success criteria; explaining the 'why' and curating positive, curious spaces made the biggest difference in real changes. (6 mentions)
- Where readiness has been built over time, even radical change meets little resistance provided staff were involved throughout; involving staff early in how AI is used works better than informing them afterwards, and ownership through involvement is distinct from making the strategic decision. (6 mentions)
- Some teams place high value on sharing knowledge openly and being open-source. (4 mentions)
- The debate about AI in media is held in binaries, replacement or not, made with AI or not, and says too little about how people and AI should work together. What matters is how AI is used, and a tool that sharpens an idea and shows its owner what they do not see is given as an example of AI helping a person, not replacing one. (4 mentions)
- The biggest cultural barrier is that newsrooms are built as accuracy machines and lack 'permission to be wrong', whereas innovation requires testing hypotheses that may fail; rigorous AI literacy turns fear into agency, and an organisation cannot assume every employee will move at the same pace. (3 mentions)
- Spreading a new way of working requires evangelists who carry the message through the flow of information; exciting the top of an organisation is necessary but not sufficient, and the people dealing with AI day to day need a practical sense of what is happening. (3 mentions)
- Innovation fails on human resistance to giving up what has worked: consensus-seeking, social proof and loss aversion make the safe choice the default. Protecting radical thinking is therefore a choice-architecture problem; experiments must be low-friction, future thinking made the default, and techniques such as pre-mortems and reference-class forecasting used against optimism bias.
- The word 'innovation' can be absent from an organisation's vocabulary or actively resented on the newsroom floor as consultant-speak; abstract, jargon-heavy packaging triggers instant scepticism, even where the underlying substance framed around concrete needs is welcome.
- Internal innovation processes are not designed to create habits and momentum, lacking triggers, variable rewards and investment loops, so people engage only when it fits their existing mental model; the gaming industry is an underrated case of deep user involvement and forced iteration.
- In principle everyone can be an innovator; organisations should involve as many people as possible, identify early innovators and give them mandate and resources.
- What matters in internal communication is feeling seen; an obviously AI-generated, generic reply from a colleague reads as not having been looked at.
- A tool that challenges is put down when its friction makes the user feel stupid: conclusions drawn too fast and provocation that seems deliberate frustrate and leave nothing to act on. Questions that guide the user towards something they can do are taken up.
