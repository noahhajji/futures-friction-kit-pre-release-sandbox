# Organisational memory

## Practices

- Individuals, including a growing number of managers, already maintain their own personal AI knowledge bases, task systems and markdown note structures, fed continuously with their own notes; this is individual initiative rather than organisational provision, and some such setups fall out of use. (17 mentions)
- Self-built shared knowledge bases become disorganised over time, making it hard to know whether an answer already exists or must be re-derived.
- Some organisations report strong success with internal AI-based knowledge-sharing practices and archive whole strategy processes as onboarding material for new managers.

## Constraints

- Organisations have no central place or knowledge of where things are stored, data is fragmented across tracking tools, no organisation-wide AI system connects content, and AI has deliberately not been connected to internal systems for security, privacy and rights reasons; escalation paths for connecting AI to internal databases are unclear. (8 mentions)
- People across an organisation independently build near-identical solutions, dashboards and fixes, tweaked slightly each time; repeats go unrecognised because of turnover and changed ways of working, and distilling what actually works across repeated attempts would be valuable. (6 mentions)
- Objectives are set for projects but not stored systematically: no database links a project to its stated objectives and outcomes, follow-up on a released concept ends in a manager's slide deck, tagging depends on people doing it, and tactical and strategic process knowledge is rarely captured even though that is where practitioners change course. (6 mentions)
- A knowledge bank of organisational processes and culture is hard to keep current because both change dynamically; it becomes a technical project needing maintenance, gives an advantage only as long as someone feeds it, and raises the question of who may edit its files. (6 mentions)
- Organisations lack structured knowledge-sharing and rarely evaluate projects afterwards; lessons from larger projects are not carried forward, hypothesis-driven learning stays inside one team, and everyday busyness paradoxically stops teams sharing what works right when they are most successful. (4 mentions)
- Search tools operate only over the article archive; almost no equivalent internal organisational-knowledge layer exists, and organisations increasingly want a general 'organisation brain' covering handbook, history and operational knowledge, on which personalised layers could sit. (4 mentions)
- Shelved ideas are handled arbitrarily: some managers track them in personal workflows, some note them in a large management document, some park them on a frozen list; parking is an individual responsibility with no shared system, so knowledge is lost. (4 mentions)
- No standard or supplier is established for an organisation's AI knowledge base, and AI companies compete to have theirs become the most used. That raises two questions for a newsroom: how it keeps its independence, and how it keeps its knowledge portable between tools. Some answer by building on open source and staying independent of any one technology company. (4 mentions)
- Institutional history can only be created by someone taking time to tell it, to colleagues or to a system, and everyday work pressure does not leave room for that; any solution must make it easy for every department.
- An organisation's own culture and structural constraints, such as shift-roster rules, should be part of the knowledge a tool works from, and any output it generates should be correctly sourced rather than fished from memory.
- An earlier attempt to build a knowledge graph over an article archive stalled because the technology at the time was too complicated.
- If an assistant does not understand the organisation's infrastructure, information gets lost; its instructions must be equipped to convey that infrastructure.

## Needs

- Organisations want to log which internal experiments worked and which did not, and to build a shared 'innovation bank' or best-practice repository that can be pinged during a meeting and consulted for what has worked elsewhere, since this knowledge is currently scattered. (6 mentions)
- An organisational 'second brain' would need to account for what was not said and for how it updates over time, since feeding it more can confuse rather than enrich; the label itself is a false analogy, and the only way to learn what such a system is for is to build one and see where it breaks. (6 mentions)
- Practitioners imagine giving an assistant access to internal communication channels to detect informal success signals, and propose capturing ideas at different steps depending on when they enter a shared knowledge base. (2 mentions)
- Organisations want an assistant with access to internal channels that proactively surfaces what other teams are doing and what works, replicating a colleague's tacit judgement, since their real challenge is cross-team context sharing rather than generating ideas.

## Beliefs

- Institutional knowledge, not only published output, is a core organisational asset: processes, meetings, chats, decisions, roles, tacit knowledge and reporters' notes. Making it accessible to people and to AI systems is seen as the next major step, and much of it currently sits trapped in individuals or in time. (13 mentions)
- Markdown is recommended as the best immediate format for institutional knowledge because it is human- and machine-readable and technology-independent; a first knowledge layer need not be a complex knowledge graph. (4 mentions)
- Practitioners need to see intermediate reasoning and patterns themselves rather than only an AI summary, because confident conclusions across differing inputs require tracing how the analysis got there; AI supports structuring and output, while lived understanding of the organisation and pattern recognition remain human.
- Much 'tacit' knowledge is technically written down, for instance in prompts, but effectively tacit because no one knows where to look; individuals run many separate system prompts before any shared context exists.
- Most innovation efforts start by pitching an exciting new solution rather than first making the organisation's existing tacit knowledge visible, which would give people a path to act instead of starting from scratch.
- Innovation arises where people meet; a knowledge base intersecting with existing domain knowledge could work the same way, generating ideas where established knowledge meets something unfamiliar.
- Knowledge bases built for a whole organisation's AI use are at a very early stage: a few newsrooms have begun to think about them, and the discussion is expected to look different within half a year. A set of files is enough while the base is small. At the scale of a whole organisation it is not, and questions of ontology, a knowledge graph on top, supplier choice, speed and cost follow.
- A personal knowledge base has to fit the person who keeps it and cannot be copied from a colleague; each person ends up finding their own way of working with it. A skill is different, because it is built to do one thing and can be shared as it is. Those who keep such a base describe it as taking mental load off them.
