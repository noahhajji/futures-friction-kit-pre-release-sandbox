# AI adoption and literacy

## Practices

- Individuals in technical, data or development roles have started building AI workflows for the business on their own initiative, often because their role puts them close to data or platforms. (9 mentions)
- Shadow AI use is widespread and often a workaround for sanctioned tools that lack system access or capability: journalists build and share their own custom GPTs, sales teams use whatever they like, marketing staff prefer external tools, and some paste restricted data into public tools. (7 mentions)
- Organisations run staged AI-literacy surveys starting with senior management to signal that AI is an organisation-wide matter, map tools and tedious tasks, reinforce staff curiosity rather than mandating courses, and expect technical material that works for a small literate group to confuse a wider audience. (6 mentions)
- Experienced users treat context as the working material. They keep track of what the model knows and does not know at each point, expect more from supplying the organisation's own knowledge than from a better model, and want a tool to spend its first exchanges asking for context before it advises. (4 mentions)
- Ideas travel between organisations at industry events; leadership groups find role-based customisation of AI tools compelling. (2 mentions)
- Coding productivity with AI agents is reported to improve by half or more; whether this means fewer developers or the same people doing far more is contested, and automation of heavy manual review work is framed as freeing staff time rather than replacing them.
- Where a good sanctioned tool exists, a large majority of editorial staff use it regularly, especially when they can tailor prompts to their own needs.
- Research on generative-AI adoption across organisations finds similar patterns, including reliance on internal ambassadors to explain the technology.
- People who use assistants daily report reading less of what they are given: long answers are skimmed rather than read, and short, structured ones are preferred. Where the tool itself suggests the user's next reply, accepted with one keystroke, the answer is skipped altogether and the user is out of the loop, which removes the friction from the exchange.

## Constraints

- Most media organisations are not set up for the newer ways of extending an AI assistant. Many are held to the single office assistant they already have, the one in which least can be done; installing a skill defeats even experienced users; and code-hosting platforms are a hill to climb for anyone non-technical, so one address to connect to is far easier to adopt. (5 mentions)
- Responsibility for adopting new tools and processes is unclear; where no one owns adoption, use fragments, and access decisions get made informally by individual managers. (4 mentions)
- Every new AI tool or feature increases complexity and load on the functions that handle security, integration, support and adoption; IT work has become far more reactive as employees introduce tools on their own initiative.
- There is no shared understanding of basic AI concepts inside organisations; asking colleagues to define an agent produces many different answers.
- AI initiatives reach good results on the first seventy per cent quickly, but reaching a quality level that can replace human work takes far more tuning than expected, delaying strategic value by a year or more.
- Because chat assistants write so fluently that their text cannot be told from a person's, users find it strange when another AI tool makes ordinary errors, although generative AI is never perfect. The fluency has blurred the understanding of AI as a tool, and people have to be pushed to think differently about new products.

## Needs

- Media organisations need someone able to follow developments at AI-native companies and frontier labs and translate them into the organisation's own environment.
- Technology is accelerating faster than people and organisations adapt; employees need time, permission and practical opportunities to develop a mental model of how AI can improve their work.

## Beliefs

- Organisations and their staff perceive internal AI adoption to be more widespread and mature than it actually is; the impression is skewed by highly visible AI-engaged voices, and leadership often initially rejects a more sober assessment. (7 mentions)
- Getting people to use new tools requires building a supporting culture and deliberate rollout choices such as workshops and demonstrations, not simply handing over the tool. (5 mentions)
- General AI assistants go straight to a solution. They are not built to raise questions or to deal with ambiguity: they assume from whatever context they hold, and what the user overlooked they decide on the user's behalf. People who work with them every day describe this as a weakness, and value a question that shows them what they had taken for granted. (5 mentions)
- AI is used for innovation because it is a major accelerator for getting things done, but the more basic work is understanding data activation across the organisation first; organisations discuss needing to become interoperable with AI systems to stay relevant yet are not ready.
- Cross-organisation collaborations on AI have been rewarding but depend on someone consistently taking the lead.
- The industry splits into three camps: those still debating whether they may use basic tools, 'tool collectors' who bolt many AI tools onto legacy workflows for efficiency, and a small group of 'rebuilders' redesigning workflows and roles from first principles.
- Early adopters are estimated to make up only three to five per cent of an organisation or market.
- Democratising AI and innovation requires more than access to technology: education, recognising that editorial staff have the ideas, easy platforms, ownership, human competencies, psychological safety, knowledge-sharing and integrated user feedback.
- Newsrooms have many unknown unknowns, and AI is weak at finding radically new ideas; it is better at synthesising and at brainstorming up to a point. In early idea work it carries a second risk: what an assistant has once put in front of a team cannot be unseen, and can corner the work onto a path that may not be the right one.
